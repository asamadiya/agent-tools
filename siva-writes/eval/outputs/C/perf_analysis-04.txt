Ten percent of CPU in page-fault stacks

A colleague of mine, Vadim, was reading a CPU flame graph from a TensorFlow workload and picked out
an orange kernel tower that had no business being there: page-fault handling, 10% of all CPU time on
the box. Ten percent of one instance is not, by itself, an emergency, but the workload runs across
thousands of instances, and a tenth of the CPU across a fleet that size is an expensive thing to be
spending on bookkeeping. We will open a flame graph over a single percentage point, so ten was going
to get looked at.

The faulting stacks bottomed out in __memcpy_avx_unaligned(), which means memcpy was touching data
segment mappings that were not resident. Early in a process's life that is exactly what you expect,
because the segments and the heap have no mappings behind them yet and every first touch costs a
fault. This process had been up for hours. By then the fault-driven growth of RSS should have
flattened out, and a process that is still paying fault costs hours in is either mapping new memory
or losing the mappings it already had.

The standard explanation for the first case is a process cycling memory through mmap() and munmap(),
which was easy enough to check. eBPF tracing with mmapsnoop.py recorded no such calls at all, which
ruled that out and left the second case.

A flame-graph search on madvise put the madvise() path at 0.8% of CPU, small enough to miss by eye
but visible once searched for, and the path ran through zap_page_range(). That is where the faults
were being manufactured. Reading mm/madvise.c settled which flag was responsible: MADV_DONTNEED is
the one that sends madvise() into zap_page_range() to tear mappings down. Tracing the flags argument
to sys_madvise() with a kprobe or a tracepoint would have answered the same question, and would have
been the better move on a kernel whose source you do not have open in another window.

The diagnosis is an optimization that ran backwards. The allocator was telling the kernel it no
longer needed pages that the workload was in fact still using; each MADV_DONTNEED threw away the
virtual-to-physical mapping for those pages, and the next touch took a fault to put the mapping
back. The 0.8% spent in madvise() was buying the 10% spent in the fault handler, which is a poor
trade even before you count the TLB and the zeroing.

The allocator turned out to be jemalloc, pulled in by a configure option that nobody had thought
hard about for this workload. Vadim rebuilt against glibc's allocator and the problem cleared. The
first round of testing came back at 3%, against the 10% we started with, and the fixed flame graph
shows the tower gone. That is one round of measurement on one configuration, not a fleet rollout,
and the remaining 3% has not been chased.

The transferable part is not jemalloc, which is a good allocator that was making a reasonable
guess about a workload it guessed wrong about. It is that a 0.8% entry on a flame graph can be the
cause of a 10% one, and that the search box is how you find it.
