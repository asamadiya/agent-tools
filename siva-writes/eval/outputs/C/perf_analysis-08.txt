## What frame pointers actually cost

With frame pointers compiled into everything we run -- libc and the JVM both, not just the application code we own -- the overhead normally came in under 1%, and on most of the services we rolled it out to it was too small to measure at all, in the sense that the difference between the frame-pointer build and the baseline was inside the run-to-run variance we see from the same binary on the same instance type. That is the headline number from the Netflix rollout and from the many overhead tests we ran alongside it. One workload broke that pattern and came in at 10%, which is worth going through in detail, because the reasons a workload lands at 10% instead of under 1% turn out to be more interesting than the average.

The 10% workload was an unusual service whose stack traces ran more than 1000 frames deep, generated through Groovy, and deep enough that Linux's perf profiler could not walk them. That is not a figure of speech about perf being slow: the stacks exceeded what perf would collect, and Arnaldo Carvalho de Melo at Red Hat added a new sysctl, kernel.perf_event_max_stack, specifically so that this one Netflix workload could be profiled at all. The instance was also a virtual machine with no low-level hardware profiling available, which meant no PMCs and no cycle analysis, so we never established that the entire 10% came from frame pointers as opposed to some other difference between the two builds. I would treat that 10% as an upper bound on the frame pointer cost for that service and not as a measurement of it.

How much this costs you depends on your workload, and the range in the reports I have seen is narrow enough to plan against. Independent reports from outside Netflix have come in at roughly 1% and roughly 2%. Those are real application workloads on real traffic, and they bracket the number I would budget for if you are turning this on across a fleet and want a figure to put in a capacity review: call it 1 to 2% worst case, under 1% typical, with the caveat that a service with pathological stack depth is a different animal and deserves its own measurement before anyone commits to a number.

Microbenchmarks sit at the bad end of that range and can show 10%, which is unsurprising once you think about what a microbenchmark is. A microbenchmark usually reduces to one small function executed in a loop, the loop is hot, the function stays resident, and any instructions added to that function risk pushing it past L1 cache warmth or over a cache line boundary, at which point throughput drops by an amount that has nothing to do with the cost of a push and a pop. The prologue and epilogue are two instructions; the cache effect is the whole result. Judging a microbenchmark result here calls for observability analysis -- cycles, instructions, PMU counters, PEBS -- and not the headline percentage the harness prints, because the headline percentage is exactly the thing that will mislead you.

One more outlier came out of Python, and it has a clean root cause worth reading as a template for how to chase these. scimark_sparse_mat_mult also touched 10%. Andrii Nakryiko at Meta ran it down to a single large function in which gcc had switched from %rsp-relative addressing to offsets computed off %rbp, and the %rbp form needs more bytes to encode, so the function grew in a way that mattered. That is a compiler codegen problem wearing a frame-pointer costume. It has since been fixed, and the fix is what lets Python turn frame pointers back on by default.

Set those costs against what frame pointers buy. The performance wins we find with working stack traces run from 5% to 500%, and a wins distribution with that shape pays for a cost under 1%, or even 1 to 2%, many times over on the first real finding. Zero overhead would be better, and later technologies may get there; SFrame and similar unwinding schemes are aimed at exactly this. For now, nothing available finds those wins as reliably as a profiler that can walk every stack on the box, and I would rather pay 1% and see the whole system than pay nothing and profile blind.
