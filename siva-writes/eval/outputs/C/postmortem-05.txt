Remediation

On 20 February 2026 a cleanup sub-task passed pending_delete with no value, and the BYOIP pipeline withdrew roughly 1,100 customer prefixes and removed their service bindings. We are sorry. Three workstreams follow, and we have ordered them by how directly each one would have stopped that sub-task.

First, we are standardizing the API schemas these internal calls use. pending_delete travelled as an untyped string, which means neither the calling client nor the receiving server could reason about what an absent value should select. A stricter schema moves that question out of the caller's head and into something tooling can check: a call that omits a required selector is malformed and gets rejected before it reaches the authoritative database. This does not make a well-formed dangerous call safe. It makes the class of call that caused this outage impossible to express.

Second, we are separating operational state from configured state. Today a customer's configuration change and an internal operational action both write to the same authoritative database, and that is why recovery on 20 February meant restoring database snapshots instead of reconciling desired state against actual state. We will snapshot what we read from that database and apply to production, then roll those snapshots out the way we ship binaries -- staged across the fleet, gated on health metrics that halt a rollout that looks wrong, and revertible to a known-good version for one customer or for all customers in near-real-time. The tradeoff is worth stating plainly, because customers will feel it: while a rollout is halted, direct API updates to configuration are blocked for the duration. Traffic keeps being served. We would rather a customer wait to change their configuration than watch their prefixes go dark.

Third, we are building a circuit breaker on rate and breadth of change. A single sub-task withdrawing 1,100 prefixes is not a plausible operator intent, and nothing in the path questioned it at the time. The new monitoring flags an unusual rate or breadth of change -- a burst of BGP prefix withdrawals or deletions is the first signal we are wiring up -- and halts snapshot deployment when it fires. Signals from our direct monitoring of customer services can trip the same breaker, so a deployment that looks healthy by its own metrics can still be stopped by what it is doing to customers.

The recovery work taught us something about blast radius that the remediation now has to carry. Affected prefixes fell into three classes. Prefixes that were only withdrawn came back with a dashboard toggle, and customers could do that themselves. Prefixes that were withdrawn and lost some service bindings recovered partially, with the missing bindings rebuilt by hand. Prefixes that were withdrawn and lost all service bindings needed a global configuration push to every edge machine, which is the most expensive recovery operation we have. The cost of an erroneous deletion is not one number. It depends on how much of the binding state went with the prefix, and the deletion path gave us no control over that.

All three workstreams sit under Code Orange: Fail Small, the company-wide effort we have staffed to bound the radius of any single change. Fail Small has three buckets: controlled rollouts for any configuration change that propagates across the network, break-glass procedures with no circular dependencies on the systems they recover, and well-defined behaviour under all failure modes rather than under the ones we anticipated. We will report progress against each bucket.
