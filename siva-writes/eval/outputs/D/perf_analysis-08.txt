Frame pointer overhead: what it actually costs

Compiling frame pointers into everything costs under 1% on production workloads, and every number we saw above that came from a specific, identifiable cause that was not a general frame pointer tax.

The Netflix rollout is the measurement that matters here, because it covered libc and Java together: with frame pointers compiled into the whole stack, overhead normally landed under 1%, and on many services it was small enough that we could not separate it from run-to-run noise at all. That last part is worth stating precisely. "Too small to measure" is a claim about the measurement setup, not about the machine, and it means the effect sat below whatever variance that service showed across deploys of identical code. It is a weaker claim than "0.3%", and it is the honest one for most of the fleet.

One workload broke the pattern at 10%, and we were never able to attribute that 10% to frame pointers. The service generated stack traces through Groovy that ran more than 1000 frames deep, deep enough that Linux's perf profiler could not walk them -- Arnaldo Carvalho de Melo at Red Hat added a new sysctl, kernel.perf_event_max_stack, specifically so that this one Netflix workload could be profiled at all. The instance was also a virtual machine with no low-level hardware profiling exposed, which left us with no PMU counters, no PEBS, and therefore no way to run cycle attribution and show where the 10% went. A 1000-frame stack is exactly the shape where frame pointer walking should cost the most, since the unwinder touches one cache line per frame, but "should" is doing real work in that sentence and we have no cycles to back it.

Microbenchmarks sit at the bad end of the range and routinely show 10%, which is what you should expect from them. A microbenchmark reduces to one small function executed in a loop, so the function body is resident in L1 instruction cache across every iteration and the prologue and epilogue are a large share of the instructions retired. Adding a push and a register move to that function risks pushing it past L1 warmth or across a cache line boundary, and throughput drops at the boundary crossing, not proportionally to the added bytes. This is why a microbenchmark number is not a fleet number, and why judging one calls for cycles, instructions, PMU counters, PEBS -- the observability work -- ahead of the headline percentage.

Python produced the one 10% result that was fully run down, and the cause was an instruction encoding artifact. scimark_sparse_mat_mult regressed by roughly 10%, and Andrii Nakryiko at Meta traced it to a single large function where gcc, with frame pointers enabled, switched from %rsp-relative addressing to offsets computed off %rbp. The %rbp form needs more bytes to encode the same accesses, and in a function with many of them the code grew enough to cost real throughput. That compiler behavior has since been fixed, which is what let Python turn frame pointers back on by default. Note the shape of this finding: the overhead was not the two instructions in the prologue, it was a downstream register allocation decision that the prologue triggered, and only cycle-level attribution found it.

Independent reports from outside Netflix have come in at roughly 1% and roughly 2%, which is the range to plan against for a mixed workload. How much it costs depends on the workload, and the spread between under 1% and 2% is real variation across code shapes rather than measurement disagreement: a service dominated by deep call chains of small functions pays more than one dominated by long-running loops inside large functions, for the same reason the microbenchmarks pay the most.

Set 1 to 2% against what frame pointers find, which in practice has been performance wins in the range 5% to 500%. A cost of 1 to 2% is repaid by a single 5% win on one service, and the 500% end of that range is a rewrite-scale result that nobody finds without a working profiler. Turn frame pointers on. When a workload reports more than a couple of percent, get PMU access on that instance and attribute the cycles before charging it to frame pointers, because in the two cases above where anyone could do that, the answer was an encoding change and an unwinder limit. Zero overhead would be better, and later unwinding technologies may deliver it; nothing available today finds those wins as reliably.
