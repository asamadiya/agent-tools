Prioritising latency work by the concurrency the tail holds

Rank latency work by the share of mean latency each slice of requests contributes, because Little's law makes that same share the fraction of your fleet's concurrency those requests occupy: in the worked example below, the slowest 1% of requests hold 52% of it.

The quantity to compute is the empirical Lorenz curve of latency. L(P) is the fraction of total mean latency contributed by requests faster than percentile P, so 1-L(P) is the fraction contributed by requests at or above P. From n raw samples it is one line: L(P) = sum(sorted(x)[:k]) / sum(x), with k = P*n. The computation assumes nothing about the shape of the distribution.

Take quantiles [1, 10, 200, 10000, 20000] ms measured at percentiles [0, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99, 0.999]. Then 1-L evaluates to [1, 0.9938, 0.9308, 0.5171, 0.1034]. Requests at or above p50 carry 99.4% of mean latency, requests at or above p99 carry 51.7%, and requests at or above p99.9 still carry 10.3%. One request in a hundred produces half the average request-time in this service.

Little's law converts that share into hardware. In a threaded service with arrival rate lambda and mean residence time W, the mean number of busy threads is lambda*W, and each request contributes to that occupancy in proportion to how long it holds its thread -- the same duration-weighting the Lorenz curve computes. If 1-L(p) = k, then 100k% of busy threads at any instant are occupied by requests that will finish at or above the pth percentile. At 1-L(0.99) = 0.5171, halving p99-and-above latency returns roughly 26% of the fleet's thread-seconds, and the same 26% of the servers bought to supply them. Values of 1-L(0.99) above 0.5, and often above 0.75, are common in services we have measured.

Compute 1-L from sorted raw samples, not from a published quantile vector. A five-point vector forces you to interpolate between the quoted percentiles and extrapolate past the last one, and a power-law fit for the tail is one option among several defensible ones. The mass sits exactly in the extrapolated region: everything beyond p99.9 in the example above is the fit speaking, not the data. Keep a reservoir sample of raw per-request durations -- 10,000 samples per service per interval is enough to place 1-L(0.99) to within a few points -- and compute the curve from that.

Two constraints bound the estimate, and both bite hardest where the answer matters most. The tail index alpha must exceed 1, or the mean latency is not estimable and 1-L is a ratio of two divergent sums; check the fitted alpha before quoting a number. Measured percentiles are themselves estimates whose uncertainty grows with the percentile: p99.9 drawn from 10,000 samples rests on about 10 observations, so its contribution to 1-L carries a wide interval and should be reported with one.

The decision this changes is which work the team funds first. A latency project that moves the p99 and above is a capacity project whose return is 1-L(0.99) times the fractional latency improvement, and that return is directly comparable in dollars to buying servers; a project that shaves 20% off the p50 in the example above moves 0.62% of mean latency and returns almost nothing. Publish 1-L(0.50), 1-L(0.99) and 1-L(0.999) per service alongside the percentiles the team already publishes. When a service reports 1-L(0.99) above 0.5, treat its tail as a capacity line item and size the fix against the hardware it frees.
