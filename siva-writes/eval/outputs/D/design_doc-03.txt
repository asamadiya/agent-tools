Request queuing for the multi-tenant RPC service

We propose a fixed array of queues in which each authorized customer id maps to a subset of two queues, each request enqueues into the shorter of that customer's two, and the mapping is perturbed on a fixed period. This holds O(1) queues and O(1) work per enqueue and per dequeue while still giving a noisy customer's requests their own delay, instead of the O(customers) queues and O(customers) round-robin sweep that per-customer fair queuing costs.

Fair queuing isolates customers by construction, and the construction is what we cannot afford verbatim. It keeps one FCFS queue per conversation and services the queues bit-by-bit round-robin, so a conversation that sends more than its fair share fills its own queue and absorbs the resulting delay and packet loss itself. The same mechanism carries over to RPC if we key the queue on authorized customer id instead of on a source-destination address pair. The cost is the part that does not carry over: a service with ten thousand active customers needs ten thousand queues, and the scheduler touches all of them on each pass to find the next eligible one.

Stochastic fairness queuing, published by Paul E. McKenney in 1990, buys back both terms with a hash. Allocate a fixed number of queues, hash the customer id into that array, and service the array round-robin. Queue count is now a configuration constant instead of a function of tenancy, the scheduler's sweep is over that constant, and enqueue is one hash. Isolation survives in the common case because a well-behaved customer that lands alone in a queue is unaffected by a flood landing in some other queue.

A hash is a permanent assignment, and that is the defect SFQ has to answer for. Two customers that collide under a fixed hash collide on every request for the lifetime of the process, so the quiet one of the pair receives half a queue's service forever and has no way to escape. McKenney's remedy is to perturb the hash function periodically: a pair that shares a queue in one period is unlikely to share one in the next, which converts a permanent penalty into a bounded one that averages out across periods.

Mapping each customer to a subset of queues and enqueuing into the shortest of the subset makes the collision itself rare, not merely short-lived. With eight queues and a subset of two, there are 28 distinct pairs, so two customers drawn at random share a full subset with probability 1/28 and share exactly one queue with probability 12/28. Sharing one queue costs the quiet customer nothing here, because best-of-two sends its request to the other queue as soon as the noisy customer's backlog makes the shared queue longer. The victim needs both of its queues occupied at once before it feels anything, and periodic perturbation of the subsets keeps even that pairing from being permanent.

The isolation holds only while a small portion of customers are noisy, and we should state the boundary rather than discover it. If enough customers are simultaneously over their share, every queue is long, best-of-two has no shorter queue to pick, and the scheme degrades toward undifferentiated round-robin across a fixed array. We have no measurement of what fraction our traffic actually puts in that state; the argument above is structural, and the fraction is the first thing to instrument.

Build it as a library that takes the queue count, the subset size, and the perturbation period as parameters. The same three parameters describe a single host choosing among its worker queues and a fleet choosing among hosts, so one implementation covers in-process admission control and cross-host load balancing. Start with eight queues per host, subset size two, and a perturbation period of one minute, then move the parameters once we can see the noisy fraction.
