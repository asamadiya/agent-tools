38% of system CPU in a filesystem nobody had mounted

A metrics ingestion team at Netflix updated their base OS image and then reported that ZFS was eating over 30% of their CPU capacity. The engineer they pulled in had worked on ZFS internals at Sun Microsystems, and he did not believe it. Unless the filesystem is badly misconfigured, ZFS does not burn that much CPU. He checked anyway. Atlas, the cloud-wide monitoring tool, put 38% of CPU time in sys, a level that almost never shows up on a Netflix cloud workload.

The first move was a flame graph, not a login. Instead of SSHing into an instance for mpstat(1) and perf(1), he used Netflix's flame graph fetch tooling -- Vector then, FlameCommander later -- and read the profile from his browser. Two orange kernel towers stood in the output, one on the left and one on the right. The left tower was arc_reclaim_thread. The right tower ran through spl_kmem_cache_reap_now(), a second ZFS memory-freeing path that was probably contending on the same locks as the first.

Both towers belong to the ARC, the Adaptive Replacement Cache that serves as ZFS's main in-memory cache. arc_reclaim_thread calls arc_adjust() to evict buffers and hold the system above a free-memory threshold. It runs periodically, and it also runs when the kernel wakes it under memory pressure. So the profile was consistent with a machine whose cache was being asked to give memory back, repeatedly and at cost.

One known cause fit the shape and failed on the numbers. A tiny ZFS record size, 512 bytes, produces millions of small buffers, and eviction over millions of buffers will light up arc_reclaim_thread exactly this way. The default record size is 128 Kbytes, three orders of magnitude larger, and nobody tunes below 8 Kbytes. A team that had just bumped a base image had no reason to have touched the property at all.

The second theory came from what else was new. An infrastructure project at the company was using ZFS to back containers, and a workload that churns containers churns filesystems underneath them. The team's answer closed that door twice. They were not running containers. Then, asked more plainly, they said they were not using ZFS at all.

Two commands settled it on the instances themselves. df -h, mount and zfs list showed no ZFS filesystem mounted, on two separate hosts. Read /proc/spl/kstat/zfs/arcstats yourself on such a box and the counters tell the same story: hits 0, misses 0, every field at zero. A cache with zero hits and zero misses has never been asked to store anything, and it was still costing 38% of the CPU.

Reading the ZFS source and its change history explained how a zero-sized cache gets expensive. A performance feature had split the ARC's eviction lists into a multilist, one list per CPU, to cut lock contention between reclaiming threads. Eviction then has to choose which of those lists to walk, and the choice was made with a cryptographically-secure random number generator. That is what the flame graph was showing underneath arc_adjust(): the stacks descended into get_random_bytes() and extract_entropy().

The bug was a loop that did real work to discover it had nothing to do. ARC detected low system memory on a host where ZFS held no data, drew entropy to select one of the per-CPU lists at random, found the list empty, and repeated. Every counter in arcstats stayed at zero while the entropy path did the spending. The report went upstream as ZFS issue #6531, and the first fix made arc_reclaim_thread bail out earlier when ZFS was unused, before paying for the random selection.

Three things about the method are worth carrying into the next investigation. The engineer's disbelief was correct and did not stop him from measuring, which is the only way disbelief stays useful. The flame graph named the offending function in one fetch, without a shell on the instance and without knowing in advance what to sample. And the users' own account of their workload -- no containers, no ZFS -- was the most surprising evidence in the case, so verify it with df and arcstats rather than treating it as the end of the search.
