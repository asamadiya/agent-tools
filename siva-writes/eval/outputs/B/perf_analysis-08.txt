## What frame pointers cost

With frame pointers compiled into everything -- libc, the JVM, and the application code on top of them -- the cost at Netflix normally came in under 1%. On most services we could not measure it at all: the difference between the two builds sat inside the run-to-run variation of the workload itself, and no amount of repetition pulled it out. That is the result we carried into the rollout decision. One service out of the fleet broke the pattern and came in at 10%.

That service was unusual in a way worth describing before anyone generalizes from it. Its stack traces ran more than 1000 frames deep, generated through Groovy, deep enough that Linux's perf profiler could not walk them at all. Arnaldo Carvalho de Melo at Red Hat added a new sysctl, `kernel.perf_event_max_stack`, specifically so that this one Netflix workload could be profiled. The instance was also a virtual machine with no low-level hardware profiling available. Without cycle analysis we could not establish that the entire 10% came from frame pointers.

Independent reports outside Netflix have landed at roughly 1% and roughly 2%. The spread is real and it tracks the workload: a program that spends its time inside a few long-running functions pays the prologue and epilogue almost never, and a program that returns from small functions in a tight loop pays them on every call. Quoting one number as the overhead of frame pointers hides that range. Measure your own service and report what you measured.

Microbenchmarks sit at the bad end of the range and can show 10%. This is unsurprising once you look at what a microbenchmark is: one small function executed in a loop, with nothing else resident. Adding a handful of instructions to that function risks pushing it past L1 cache warmth or over a cache line boundary, and throughput drops at that point. The measured 10% is then a property of the boundary the function crossed, not a per-call cost that a real service would pay at the same rate.

Judging a microbenchmark result calls for observability analysis, not the headline number. Read the cycles and the instructions retired. Pull the PMU counters, the PMCs, and the PEBS samples, and find out where the added cycles went before attributing them to the extra push and pop. A 10% regression whose instruction count moved by a fraction of a percent is telling you about the front end or the cache, and the fix for it lives somewhere other than the calling convention.

Python produced the one outlier where that analysis paid off directly. The `scimark_sparse_mat_mult` benchmark also touched 10%, and Andrii Nakryiko at Meta ran it down to a large function in which gcc had moved from `%rsp`-relative addressing to offsets computed off `%rbp`. The `%rbp` form needs more bytes to encode, so the function grew. The regression came from a code generation choice that frame pointers triggered, not from the frame pointers themselves, and it has since been fixed. Python can now turn frame pointers back on by default.

Set those costs against what frame pointers find. Working profiles on production services have produced performance wins in the range 5% to 500%, because a profiler that can walk every stack shows you the caller that is actually responsible for the CPU time. A cost under 1% buys that, and so does a cost of 1 to 2%. The arithmetic only fails if you expect the profiles to find nothing.

Zero overhead would be better, and later unwinding technologies may reach it. None of them is the reason to leave frame pointers off today. Nothing currently available finds those wins as reliably across a mixed-language stack, on a virtual machine, with no PMU and with symbols you do not control. Turn them on, measure your own overhead against your own noise floor, and spend the 1%.
