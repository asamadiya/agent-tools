Design doc: pricing latency work with the latency Lorenz curve

Status: proposal. Owner: Capacity and Performance.

The decision

Every quarter we choose between buying machines and making slow requests faster, and we argue that choice on percentile targets that carry no cost term. A p99 of 10 s is a fact about one request in a hundred. It says nothing about how much hardware those requests are holding. This document proposes that we compute the latency Lorenz curve for each tier and use it as the conversion between a percentile and the fraction of the fleet that percentile occupies.

The measure

The empirical Lorenz curve L(P) gives the share of total mean latency contributed by requests faster than percentile P, so its complement 1-L(P) is the share contributed by requests at or above P. Compute it from sorted raw samples: with n samples and k = P*n, L = sum(sorted(x)[:k]) / sum(x). That is exact. From a stored quantile vector you must interpolate between the recorded points and extrapolate past the last one; a power-law fit on the tail is one workable choice, and it is an estimate, not the curve.

A worked example

Take quantiles [1, 10, 200, 10000, 20000] ms recorded at percentiles [0, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99, 0.999]. The complement is 1-L = [1, 0.9938, 0.9308, 0.5171, 0.1034]. Read the fourth entry: requests at or above p99 contribute 51.71% of mean latency, and the other 99% of requests contribute 48.29% between them. The slowest one request in a thousand contributes 10.34%. Halving the p99 population's service time moves mean latency about as far as halving everything else combined.

Why this is a cost number

Little's law turns that share into occupied capacity: if 1-L(P) = k in a threaded service, then 100k% of busy threads are held by requests that will finish at or above the Pth percentile. In the example, 51.71% of busy threads serve that top 1% -- 103 of a 200-thread pool. Fleet size is set by concurrency, not by the median. Values of 1-L(0.99) above 0.5, and often above 0.75, are common in our tiers, which is why tail work and machine purchases are substitutes.

Constraints on the number

Two conditions bound what the curve proves. The tail index alpha must exceed 1 or the mean is not estimable and L is undefined, so we fit alpha before reporting and we suppress the curve when the fit returns alpha <= 1. Measured percentiles are themselves estimates, and their uncertainty grows with the percentile: p999 from a one-minute window of a few thousand requests is a handful of samples. Report 1-L(0.99) with a confidence interval from bootstrapped samples and treat anything past p999 as directional.

Proposed use

Each tier reports 1-L(0.99) and 1-L(0.999) weekly beside its QPS and instance count. Prioritise a latency investigation over a capacity request when 1-L(0.99) exceeds 0.5, because at that point the top percentile holds more than half the concurrency the fleet is provisioned for, and a fix returns hardware. Below 0.25, buy the machines and move the engineers elsewhere.

Work required

Sampling is the gap. Our metrics pipeline stores quantile vectors only, which forces interpolation at exactly the region we care about. We propose reservoir-sampling 10,000 raw latencies per tier per five-minute window, which supports an exact L up to p999 with roughly 10 samples in the last bucket, and computing the curve in the existing rollup job. Estimated cost is one additional column per tier and one batch step.

Open question: whether to weight the curve by request cost class before aggregating tiers that mix cheap and expensive endpoints.
