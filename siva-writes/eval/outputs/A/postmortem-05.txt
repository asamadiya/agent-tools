Remediation

The proximate cause of this outage was a cleanup sub-task that passed pending_delete with no value, and a system that accepted it. Roughly 1,100 BYOIP prefixes were withdrawn, and their service bindings were deleted along with them. Nothing about that call was malformed in any way our tooling could detect, because pending_delete was an untyped string: neither the client nor the server had enough information to reason about what an empty value meant. Our remediation is organized around three workstreams, and the first of them starts there.

API schema standardization. We are tightening the schemas on our internal control-plane APIs so that a field like pending_delete carries a type and a permitted range of values. This is unglamorous work and it does not, on its own, prevent an engineer from making a destructive but well-formed request. What it does is move an entire class of failure from "executed correctly against garbage input" into "rejected at the boundary," where a linter, a test, or the server itself can catch it.

Separating operational state from configured state. Today, customer configuration and operational actions write to the same authoritative database. That coupling is why recovery was slow: with no separate record of what had actually been applied, our path back ran through database snapshots rather than through a reconciliation of desired state against actual state. We are changing this. What we read from the database and apply to production will be snapshotted, and those snapshots will roll out the way we ship binaries — progressively, gated by health metrics that halt a rollout that looks wrong. The payoff is a near-instant revert, for an individual customer or for all customers, to a known-good version.

This carries a tradeoff we want to state plainly rather than discover together during the next incident. While a rollout is halted, customers are temporarily blocked from pushing configuration changes through our API directly. We have chosen that deliberately. A customer who cannot update a setting for some minutes is inconvenienced; a customer whose prefixes are withdrawn is offline. We would rather freeze the control plane than drop the traffic.

A circuit breaker. The third workstream is monitoring that watches not the correctness of a single change but the rate and breadth of change in aggregate. A burst of BGP prefix withdrawals or deletions is the signature we most want to catch, and it is exactly the signature this incident produced. When that monitoring trips, snapshot deployment halts. Signals from direct monitoring of customer services can trip the same breaker, so the trigger does not depend solely on our own view of our own control plane being accurate.

The recovery classes we observed during the incident explain why we are investing here rather than only in input validation. Prefixes that were only withdrawn came back with a dashboard toggle. Prefixes that were withdrawn with some bindings removed were partially recoverable. Prefixes that were withdrawn with all bindings removed required a global configuration push to every edge machine — the slowest and most disruptive tool we have. The difference between the first class and the third was not the quality of the bad request. It was how far that request had propagated before anything stopped it.

All three workstreams sit under Code Orange: Fail Small, our current company-wide engineering priority. Its three buckets are controlled rollouts for any configuration change that propagates across the network, break-glass procedures free of circular dependencies, and well-defined behaviour under all failure modes. This incident was a single call that propagated globally with nothing in its path. Fail Small is the commitment that it should not have been able to.
