Raw material. Three cost claims. Turning written business logic into code now costs approximately zero. Integrating services and libraries, the plumbing, also costs approximately zero. Building efficient, reliable, secure, end-to-end systems is starting to get cheaper, but slowly. Two possible readings of that moment. Reading one: the end of programming as a high-value economic craft, following weaving, ploughing, and coopering. Supporting anecdote: a mostly retired expert in analog circuit design who, in the late 1960s, founded a hardware company with friends on the bet that serious customers would reject digital logic; the 74 series had just launched and they found it graceless, unreliable, and full of problems. Measured by the ratio of transistors in digital versus analog circuits in 2026, the bet was wrong by ten or more orders of magnitude, yet he still made a small fortune, enough to keep his children comfortable, because the world changes slower than anyone would like. Reading two: new tools bring new industries, research fields, and careers. Amdahl's Law explains where the value moves: speeding up one portion of a problem makes that portion a shrinking share of total time. What remains sticky: real-world feedback loops, human stubbornness, occasional adversaries. Integration, translation, simple analysis, and small-scale synthesis are solved; genuinely new ideas are not, and grow more valuable as the lever lengthens.
