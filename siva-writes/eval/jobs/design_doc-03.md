Raw material. Paul E. McKenney published Stochastic Fairness Queuing in 1990. Baseline fairness queuing keeps a separate FCFS queue per conversation and services queues bit-by-bit round-robin; ill-behaved conversations that exceed their fair share get longer delays and higher packet loss than well-behaved ones. The same construction works for RPC traffic by keying on something else, such as authorized customer id, instead of a source-destination address pair. Cost of the baseline in a distributed system: O(customers) queues plus O(customers) work to round-robin across them. SFQ replaces per-conversation queues with a fixed set of queues and a simple hash mapping into them: O(1) queues, O(1) time. New problem: two conversations that collide keep colliding, so each persistently receives less than its share. Remedy: periodically perturb the hash function so colliders in one period are unlikely to collide in the next. A further variant combines SFQ with shuffle sharding and best-of-two: map each customer to a subset of queues (possibly just two), enqueue each request into the shortest queue in that customer's subset, and periodically perturb subsets. Properties: O(1) queues, O(1) enqueue, O(1) dequeue, strong noisy-neighbour isolation provided only a small portion of customers are noisy. Usable on a single host or across hosts for load balancing.
