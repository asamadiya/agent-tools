Raw material. Concurrency control in Aurora DSQL, a multi-region relational database. Optimistic concurrency control was chosen for throughput and for one isolation property: under it a client is never in a position to block another one. Claimed consequence at scale: client-to-client performance cross talk, a large driver of tail latency whenever an application sits on a pessimistic relational engine. Stock illustration: a process stops for garbage collection holding a write lock, and everything queued behind it stops too. Operational record from Amazon: lock contention, and the retries that follow a failed lock acquisition, turn up repeatedly as causes of outages and of metastable failure, with long recoveries. Two design objectives follow: keep applications out of metastable failure, and blunt the duller failures that come from badly behaved clients and client code. Measured result: figure 6 plots SELECT and UPDATE latency, and both stay fast even with the database running across regions. Second thread, consistency defaults. Two publications from the 2000s, the Amazon Dynamo paper (2007) and Werner Vogels' article Eventually Consistent (2009), held that cloud-scale systems had to give up strong consistency to reach their availability and latency targets. Four things have shifted since: how time is distributed, how data-centre networks are built, how power and cooling are provisioned, and what distributed protocols can do. Position taken: strong consistency now belongs as the default, and almost no application is better served by eventual consistency.
