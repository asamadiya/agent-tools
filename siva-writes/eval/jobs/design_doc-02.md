Raw material. Two disputes. A service reports mean request time of 100ms; a user reports her mean wait is 1s. A service reports MTTR under 1 minute; a user reports mean outage length of 1 hour. Both sides are right. The operator counts events (one request, one outage); the customer samples elapsed time, and the number of times a customer hits a given outage is proportional to that outage's length. This is the inspection paradox: customers do not see f(t), they see a t-weighted version. If the operator's mean is E[X], the customer-experienced mean is E_a[X] = E[X^2] / (2 E[X]) = 0.5 * (E[X] + Var(X)/E[X]). The gap is proportional to Var(X)/E[X], and Var(X) is often very large relative to E[X]. Fitted log-normal example: median TTR 30 minutes, p99 600 minutes (10 hours, one event in 100) gives MTTR just over 1 hour and a customer-experienced mean of several hours, a multiple of the MTTR rather than a correction to it. A simulator uses lognormal failure_mu=1.0, failure_sigma=3.0, Poisson client arrivals at rate 100, 1000 samples. Timeout-and-retry can hide some service-time tail, but nothing hides recovery-time tail. Write a design doc for a platform org proposing how availability and recovery should be measured.
