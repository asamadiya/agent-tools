Raw material. The empirical Lorenz curve L(P) answers: of total mean latency, how much is contributed by requests faster than percentile P. So 1-L(P) is the share contributed by requests at or above P. From raw samples: L = sum(sorted(x)[:k]) / sum(x), with n samples and k = p*n. From a quantile vector you must interpolate and extrapolate; a power-law fit is one option, exact only if you go back to sorted raw samples. Worked example: quantiles [1, 10, 200, 10000, 20000] ms measured at percentiles [0, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99, 0.999] give 1-L = [1, 0.9938, 0.9308, 0.5171, 0.1034]. Requests at or above p50 account for ~99% of mean latency; requests at or above p99 account for ~52%. Little's law makes that same fraction the share of system concurrency: in a threaded service, if 1-L(p)=k then 100k% of busy threads are occupied by requests that will finish above the pth percentile. Values of 1-L(0.99) above 0.5 or 0.75 are common. Constraints: tail alpha must exceed 1 or the mean is not estimable; measured percentiles are themselves estimates and uncertainty grows with the percentile.
