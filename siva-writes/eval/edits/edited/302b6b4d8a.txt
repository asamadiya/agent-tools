How the frame pointer went missing

The x86-64 ABI makes the frame pointer optional, and that one word of permission is where this story starts. %rbp holds the base of the current stack frame, and a profiler that wants a stack -- Linux perf, eBPF -- reads %rbp, follows the saved value to the caller's frame, and repeats until it runs out of frames. That walk is what a flame graph is drawn from. Compilers are free to skip it: index into the frame off %rsp instead, drop two instructions from every prologue and epilogue, and hand the allocator back a register.

The default that broke stack walking was set in 2004, on a machine where the register mattered. Roger Sayle patched gcc's i386 backend so that 32-bit targets compiled as though -fomit-frame-pointer -ffixed-ebp had been passed. On i386 the arithmetic was real: four allocatable general-purpose registers became five, or six to seven counting %si and %di, which is a 25% increase in what the allocator could actually spend. Sayle gave two further reasons. He wanted to beat Intel's icc on benchmarks, and he judged that the debuggers of the day would be unharmed, since they already walked stacks by other means.

The damage came from carrying that default across to x86-64. That target had more than a dozen general-purpose registers before %rbp was freed, and freeing it moves the allocatable set from 15 to 16 -- a gain of 6.7%, against 25% on i386, for the same two instructions. The prologue being saved is push %rbp followed by mov %rsp, %rbp, with the matching teardown on exit, and hardware has been optimizing that exact pair for years. Leaf routines never establish a frame at all, so they collect no saving whatsoever.

Eric Schrock, then at Sun, made the argument in November 2004 and was ignored. On amd64, he wrote, a seventeenth general-purpose register opens up no new class of compiler optimization; what you avoid is a pushl/movl sequence the hardware has long since absorbed; leaf routines were never paying the cost; and set against what you lose in debuggability, the trade hardly seemed worth making. His closing line named the failure mode before it happened: the practice goes off the rails once people begin compiling /usr/bin without frame pointers. Linux compiled /usr/bin exactly that way, and then /usr/lib, and then application code on top of both.

Count what that costs on a profile you would actually collect. The walk descends through your application's frames, reaches a libc built without %rbp, and terminates there, because the register it was following now holds something else. What comes back is a stack missing its application frames. Every such sample is truncated at roughly the same depth and carries roughly the same frames, so they sort together and stack into one tall block on the left edge of the flame graph. In one example that block is 15% of all samples -- 15% of the profile telling you nothing about your own code.

Off-CPU profiling comes off worse. An off-CPU flame graph is built from the moments a thread blocks, and the blocking calls are read, write, and mutex operations in libc -- which is to say the fraction of the profile that terminates early is not a corner of it but most of it. The tool inverts: the workload you most need the stack for is the workload least able to give you one.

Java arrived in 2014 with nothing to walk at all. When I joined Netflix that year, the JVM had no frame pointer support, and every application stack broke at the first compiled Java frame; a mixed-mode flame graph of a JVM under load was a wall of kernel and libc with the interesting half absent. I wrote a fix for the c2 compiler. Oracle reworked it and shipped it as -XX:+PreserveFramePointer in JDK8u60, which is the flag that made Java flame graphs work.

libc was the next piece, and it had to be fixed by rebuilding it. I compiled my own libc with frame pointers and ran it in production, which recovers your own stacks and nobody else's. Then I worked with Canonical on a prebuilt libc6-prof, so that getting a complete stack stopped requiring a private toolchain and became a package install. Twenty years after a patch aimed at a four-register machine, distributions are still restoring the register on the machines that never needed to give it up.
