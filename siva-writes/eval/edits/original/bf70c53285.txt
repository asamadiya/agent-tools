1.1.1.1 Resolver: 62-minute global outage on 14 July 2025

The 1.1.1.1 public Resolver was globally unreachable from 21:52 to 22:54 UTC on 14 July 2025, 62 minutes, because a configuration error made on 6 June sat dormant in our legacy topology system until an unrelated test change activated it. All times are UTC.

The 6 June error attached the Resolver's service prefixes to a non-production service. At 17:38 that day, a release preparing a service topology for a future Data Localization Suite service listed the 1.1.1.1 prefixes under that service. Nothing changed on the production network: no traffic shifted, no prefix was withdrawn, and no alert fired, because the non-production service had no locations attached and therefore generated no advertisement anywhere. The error existed only as a binding in configuration, and it stayed invisible for 38 days.

Attaching one offline test location to that non-production service withdrew every Resolver prefix worldwide. At 21:48 on 14 July an engineer attached an offline location for internal testing, which triggered a global refresh of network configuration. The legacy system hard-codes a full location list per service and republishes it, so the refresh recomputed the 1.1.1.1 topology from the non-production service's list, which contained exactly one offline location. The Resolver collapsed from every Cloudflare location to that one, and 1.1.1.0/24, 1.0.0.0/24, 2606:4700:4700::/48 and the remaining Resolver prefixes were withdrawn from BGP by 21:52.

Query volume over UDP, TCP and DoT went to near zero within seconds of the withdrawal, because those transports address 1.1.1.1 and 1.0.0.1 directly and there was no longer a route to them. DoH stayed largely stable: most DoH clients reach cloudflare-dns.com, which resolves to addresses outside the withdrawn set, and those prefixes kept their advertisements throughout. Gateway DNS, which forwards to the same Resolver addresses, was intermittently degraded across the whole 62 minutes.

At 21:54, Tata Communications India (AS4755) began advertising 1.1.1.0/24. The announcement did not cause the outage and did not extend it. A withdrawn prefix leaves any other announcement as the best available path, so the hijack became visible to the routing table at the moment our own advertisement disappeared.

Internal health alerts fired at 22:01, nine minutes after the withdrawal and thirteen minutes after the triggering change, and the incident was declared. Nine minutes is the cost of alerting on Resolver health signals instead of on the configuration change that produced them; the change itself passed peer review and generated no pre-publication signal at all.

Reverting the topology at 22:20 returned about 77% of pre-incident traffic. Re-advertising the prefixes restored service at every location whose edge servers still had the Resolver IPs bound to them. The remaining 23% of edge servers had those IP bindings removed by the same global refresh, and restoring a binding runs through change management, which normally stages over hours. We validated the restoration in test locations first and then accelerated the rollout. Traffic returned to normal at 22:54 and the incident was resolved at 22:55.

The defect is the deployment model, not the 6 June typo. A hard-coded location list gives a topology change exactly two states, correct everywhere and wrong everywhere, and republishing it applies the change to every location in one step with no health gate between the first location and the last. A canary would have caught this in one location: the first location to lose its Resolver advertisement would have shown the same query collapse that our alerts saw nine minutes later, against a global impact of one site instead of all of them. Peer review did not catch it, and peer review is not a substitute for staged deployment, because a reviewer reads intent while a canary measures effect.

We are retiring the legacy topology system. Until it is gone, service topology changes are being moved onto our standard progressive deployment mechanism, the same one that stages code: a change reaches a small set of locations first, health signals gate the next stage, and a regression halts the rollout without an operator in the loop. We are also removing the ability of a non-production service to hold prefixes that a production service advertises, so that a binding error cannot cross that boundary again.
