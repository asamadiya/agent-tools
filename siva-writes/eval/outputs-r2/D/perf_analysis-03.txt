38% of system CPU spent evicting from a cache that held nothing

A Netflix metrics-ingestion service was burning 38% of CPU time in sys after a base image update, and every bit of that time was inside the ZFS ARC eviction path on instances that had no ZFS filesystem mounted and an ARC whose counters all read zero.

The team's own diagnosis was that ZFS was eating over 30% of their CPU capacity, which is not a thing ZFS does. The engineer they pulled in had worked on ZFS internals at Sun, and his prior was that absent a bad configuration -- a record size tuned down to 512 bytes, say, against a default of 128 Kbytes and a floor of 8 Kbytes that nobody sane goes below -- the filesystem does not have a path that burns tens of percent of a box. A prior is not a measurement, though, and 38% in sys is unusual enough for Netflix cloud workloads that it wanted an explanation regardless of which subsystem turned out to own it.

Atlas, the cloud-wide monitor, is where the 38% came from, and Atlas cannot say which kernel code spends it. The flame graph answered that in one fetch, without an SSH session: rather than logging in for mpstat(1) and perf(1), he pulled a CPU flame graph through Netflix's own fetch tooling, Vector then and FlameCommander later. On a cloud fleet the cost of profiling is mostly the cost of reaching the instance, and moving that to a self-service fetch is what makes profiling the first step instead of the fourth.

The graph showed two orange kernel towers. The left one was arc_reclaim_thread, which runs arc_adjust() to evict buffers and hold a free-memory threshold, waking periodically or on a low-memory signal. The right one entered spl_kmem_cache_reap_now(), a second ZFS memory-freeing path, and the two were almost certainly contending on the same locks.

Then the machines said they had no ZFS. The first theory was a new container infrastructure project that used ZFS per container and would churn filesystems as containers came and went; the team replied that they ran no containers, and then that they ran no ZFS. df -h, mount and zfs list on two instances agreed: no ZFS filesystems. /proc/spl/kstat/zfs/arcstats agreed harder, with hits at 0, misses at 0, and every other counter at 0 as well. The module was loaded because the new BaseAMI ships it loaded. Nothing had ever been cached in it.

The cost was in the list selection, not in the eviction. Reading the source and the change history gives the mechanism: a multilist performance feature had split the ARC's eviction lists into one per CPU to cut lock contention, and the evictor picks which list to work on at random. The flame graph's leaf frames say which random -- get_random_bytes() and extract_entropy(), the cryptographically-secure generator, which costs orders of magnitude more per draw than the xorshift you would pick for choosing a bucket index. So the loop was: kernel signals low memory, ARC wakes, ARC pays for a secure random draw, ARC lands on a zero-length list, ARC finds nothing to free, ARC repeats. Every counter reading zero is what makes the shape legible. The thread did no eviction work at all, because there was nothing to evict, and the entire 38% was overhead of deciding where to look plus the lock traffic between the two towers.

Two habits fall out of this, and the first one is not "profile before you blame." That one is obvious and the engineer followed it against his own correct instinct, which is the useful part -- his instinct that ZFS cannot burn 30% of a box was right about ZFS-in-use and wrong about ZFS-at-rest, and only the flame graph distinguished those. The second is that a module loaded is a module running. The BaseAMI change enabled no feature the team asked for and the team could truthfully say they did not use ZFS, yet arc_reclaim_thread was registered on the low-memory path from boot, and any memory pressure on the box paid for it. Audit what a base image loads, not what it configures.

The upstream fix, filed as ZFS issue #6531, made arc_reclaim_thread bail out earlier when ZFS is unused, which removes the 38% without touching the multilist design or the choice of RNG. That is the right fix for the reported symptom and a narrow one: a box that does use ZFS still draws from get_random_bytes() on every eviction pass, and whether that cost is visible under a real workload is a separate measurement nobody here took.
