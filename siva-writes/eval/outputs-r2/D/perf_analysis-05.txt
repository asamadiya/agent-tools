Slow uploads: the disk is not slow, the queue is 25 deep
========================================================

The 100-Gbyte uploads were slow because the service itself kept about 25 large reads outstanding against a single device, and Little's law reproduces the observed 33 ms read latency from that queue depth alone -- no device-side slowness is needed to explain any of it. This is the first thing we established, on a 16-CPU AWS instance running 4.4.0-1072-aws in December 2018, and it is what redirected the investigation away from storage.

The report was that uploads of roughly 100-Gbyte files ran for hours while uploads of up to 40 Gbytes finished in minutes, and that the slow window lined up with a pageins spike in Atlas. Pageins count pages faulted in off disk. Plenty of healthy workloads generate them; the correlation is a place to start looking, not a finding.

`iostat -xz 1` during a large upload put device xvdb at 734 to 750 r/s, 91,656 to 92,152 rkB/s, avgrq-sz 246 to 250 sectors, avgqu-sz about 25, r_await 33 to 34 ms, and %util pegged at 100. CPU over the same seconds was about 14.8% user with 24 to 30% iowait. Dividing rkB/s by r/s gives about 124 Kbytes per read, which agrees with avgrq-sz at 512 bytes per sector, and the application is issuing large reads and blocking on them. Reads are the operations applications wait on; writes frequently are not, because write-back caching returns before the device does. With 16 CPUs, 24 to 30% iowait means four to five of them were sitting in uninterruptible sleep at any instant while 14.8% user says the encryption work was not the constraint.

A 33 ms read wait is high, and the arithmetic says where it comes from. Little's law gives mean latency as queue depth divided by completion rate: 25 / 740 per second = 33.8 ms, against a measured r_await of 33 to 34 ms. The agreement is close enough that there is nothing left over to attribute to the device. Invert it the other way and the device's own service time is 1 / 740 = 1.35 ms per read, or about 92 Mbyte/s sustained; a request arriving at a queue of 25 waits behind roughly 24 predecessors at 1.35 ms each. That is the whole 33 ms. Treat %util at 100 on a virtualized block device as "there was always at least one request in flight" rather than as a statement about spindle or NAND saturation, but the conclusion does not rest on it.

Per-second averages can hide device-level outliers. The next tool was `biolatency -m` from bcc, an eBPF histogram of block I/O completion times. The buckets: 83 I/O at 0-1 ms, 1,620 at 16-31 ms, 8,139 at 32-63 ms, 176 at 64-127 ms, 95 at 128-255 ms, 61 at 256-511 ms, 93 at 512-1023 ms. The mode sits at 32-63 ms, straddling the 33.8 ms the queue depth already predicted, and about 96% of the I/O falls in 16-127 ms. The tail out to 0.5-1.0 s is 93 operations out of roughly 10,300, under 1%, and a queue that deep produces a tail of that shape on its own. No bimodal population of slow completions is sitting under the average waiting to be found.

Size distribution was the remaining candidate for something anomalous, and `bitesize` ruled it out: process name java, 1,682 I/O in the 128-255 Kbyte bucket and only tens in every other bucket. One process owns the I/O, and it is issuing one size. That is consistent with the 124 Kbytes computed from iostat and inconsistent with, say, a pathological small-read pattern from a misconfigured buffer.

Three tools therefore agree that the device is behaving normally under a workload that is asking it for about 92 Mbyte/s with 25 requests in flight. At 92 Mbyte/s a 100-Gbyte file takes about 1,090 seconds to move once, which is 18 minutes and not hours: even the saturated read stream does not by itself account for the reported duration, and something is reading the data more than once. The question worth spending time on is not why xvdb takes 33 ms but why a Java process reading a 100-Gbyte file generates sustained disk reads at all when the 40-Gbyte case does not, which puts the page cache and the instance's memory size next on the list.
