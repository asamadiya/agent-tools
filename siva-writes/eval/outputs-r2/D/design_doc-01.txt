Ranking latency work by its share of mean latency and of threads

Requests at or above p99 carry 51.7% of mean latency in a service whose quantiles are [1, 10, 200, 10000, 20000] ms at [p0, p50, p90, p99, p99.9], and Little's law makes that the same 51.7% of busy threads, so work on that tail is a capacity purchase and should be funded and ranked as one.

The empirical Lorenz curve is the instrument that tells you which requests carry the mean. Define L(P) as the fraction of total latency contributed by requests faster than percentile P. From raw samples it is a sorted running sum: L = sum(sorted(x)[:k]) / sum(x), with n samples and k = P*n. The complement 1-L(P) is the share contributed by requests at or above P. The mean is a sum, and a request contributes to that sum in proportion to its own duration, which is why 20,000 requests of 1 ms and one request of 20,000 ms weigh the same. No distributional assumption enters this step.

For the quantile vector above, 1-L = [1, 0.9938, 0.9308, 0.5171, 0.1034] at percentiles [0, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99, 0.999]. Requests at or above p50 carry 99.38% of mean latency, which means the faster half of the traffic contributes 0.62% and cannot be optimised into anything worth a headcount. Requests at or above p99 carry 51.71%; requests at or above p99.9 carry 10.34%. Sort your own samples, take the running sum, and divide -- the calculation is three lines and answers the question directly for your service.

Little's law converts that latency share into a concurrency share. Concurrency equals arrival rate times mean latency, and the requests above percentile P arrive at a fixed fraction (1-P) of the total rate, which makes their occupancy exactly their contribution to the mean: if 1-L(P) = k, then 100k% of busy threads are occupied by requests that will finish above the pth percentile. On a 200-thread pool under this distribution, 103 threads at any instant hold a request destined for the p99-and-above bucket. Halving the latency of those requests releases about 52 threads, and released threads are servers you do not buy.

Values of 1-L(0.99) above 0.5, and often above 0.75, are common, and that is the number to put next to the cost of the work. A p99 improvement on a service at 1-L(0.99) = 0.75 pays back three times the fleet reduction of the same improvement on a service at 0.25, for the same engineering cost. Rank the backlog on 1-L(0.99) times the achievable latency reduction times fleet cost, and the ordering stops depending on whose dashboard is red this week.

Compute 1-L from sorted raw samples wherever you can get them. From a stored quantile vector you must interpolate between the published percentiles and extrapolate beyond the last one; a power-law fit to the tail is one option, and it is exact only in the sense that it reproduces the fitted points. The two steps in the worked example that carry the most weight -- the segment from p99 to p99.9 and everything past p99.9 -- are the two the quantile vector constrains least.

Two conditions bound the method. The tail exponent alpha must exceed 1, or the mean does not exist and every share above is a ratio of two diverging sums; check it before reporting a number. Measured percentiles are themselves estimates, and their uncertainty grows with the percentile: p99.9 taken from 10,000 samples rests on 10 observations. Its contribution term of 10.34% therefore carries a sampling error large enough that you should not rank two services whose 1-L(0.99) differ by less than about 0.05.
