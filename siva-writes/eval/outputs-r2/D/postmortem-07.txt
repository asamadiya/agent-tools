1.1.1.1 Resolver outage, 14 July 2025

A configuration error we made on 6 June sat inert in our topology system for 38 days, and on 14 July a routine internal test applied it globally in seconds, withdrawing every 1.1.1.1 Resolver prefix from the internet for 62 minutes. The Resolver was unavailable worldwide from 21:52 to 22:54 UTC, and Gateway DNS was intermittently degraded over the same window. All times below are UTC. We are sorry. 1.1.1.1 is infrastructure that other people build on, and a global withdrawal is the worst failure this service has.

The error was written on 6 June at 17:38, when a release prepared a service topology for a future Data Localization Suite service and attached the 1.1.1.1 Resolver prefixes -- 1.1.1.0/24, 1.0.0.0/24, 2606:4700:4700::/48 and others -- to that non-production service. Nothing observable changed. No traffic shifted, no prefix moved, and no alert fired, because the non-production service had no locations attached and therefore produced no network configuration. The prefixes kept being advertised from the production topology, which was still correct. What existed on 6 June was a latent second owner for the addresses, waiting for the moment something asked the non-production service what its locations were.

That moment arrived on 14 July at 21:48, when an engineer attached an offline test location to the non-production service for internal testing. Attaching a location triggers a global refresh of network configuration. The refresh recomputed the 1.1.1.1 topology from its new owner and found a single offline location. The prefix list collapsed from every Cloudflare location to that one, and the withdrawal propagated worldwide within four minutes. UDP, TCP and DoT query volume dropped to near zero. DoH held up, because most DoH clients resolve through cloudflare-dns.com, which sits on different addresses that were never in the affected topology.

Recovery split into two phases with very different costs. Internal health alerts fired at 22:01, nine minutes after impact began, and we declared an incident. We began reverting the topology change at 22:20; prefixes were re-advertised and traffic returned to roughly 77% of pre-incident levels within minutes, because re-advertising a prefix is one configuration action per location. The remaining 23% took another half hour: those edge servers had their 1.1.1.1 IP bindings removed as part of the same refresh, and restoring a binding runs through change management, which is normally staged over hours. We validated the restoration in test locations and then accelerated it. Traffic reached normal at 22:54 and we resolved the incident at 22:55.

At 21:54, two minutes into the outage, Tata Communications India (AS4755) began advertising 1.1.1.0/24. This was not a cause and it did not extend the outage. Our own withdrawal removed the legitimate announcement that had been suppressing the hijack, which then became visible to the routing table. If you saw 1.1.1.1 resolve to something unexpected during the window, this is the likely explanation.

The fix is not a better review of the 6 June change, because that change was peer reviewed. The legacy topology system hard-codes location lists and applies the resulting configuration to all locations at once. Two properties follow from that design. A topology can be misattached without any signal, because the system validates configuration only when something asks it to render, and a render always has global blast radius, because there is no staged or canary deployment path. Peer review is the only gate a change like this passes, and peer review does not catch an assignment that is correct in isolation and wrong against a table nobody displayed.

We are retiring the legacy system. Topology changes will move to the same progressive deployment model our other configuration uses, with health-gated stages instead of a single global apply. We are adding a validation step that rejects a prefix attached to more than one service at write time, which would have failed the 6 June release at 17:38. We are also removing the coupling between a topology refresh and edge IP bindings. A routing revert should restore 100% of traffic, not 77%.
