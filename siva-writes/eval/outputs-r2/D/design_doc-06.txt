Where to put engineering investment for the next decade

The two activities that consumed most of our engineering hours -- turning written business logic into code, and wiring services and libraries together -- now cost approximately zero, which leaves our return on an engineer sitting entirely in the work the tools did not touch.

Three costs moved by different amounts, and the spread between them is the whole of this argument. Translating a written rule into working code costs approximately zero. Integration, the plumbing between services and libraries, also costs approximately zero. Building efficient, reliable, secure, end-to-end systems is getting cheaper and it is getting cheaper slowly, with no sign that the curve is near a floor. The first two costs collapsed together; the third did not.

Amdahl's Law says what that spread does to a portfolio. Speeding up one portion of a problem makes that portion a shrinking share of total time, and driving it all the way to zero does not shrink what remains -- it promotes what remains to the whole. An organisation whose hours were dominated by translation and plumbing has not become an organisation with fewer hours of work. It has become an organisation whose every remaining hour is systems work: capacity, failure modes, security boundaries, and the seam where the software meets the world.

Three things keep that remaining portion expensive, and none of them are code-shaped. Real-world feedback loops run on the world's clock: a change to a ranking system, a pricing rule, or a control loop is evaluated by users and physics over weeks, and no tool shortens that measurement. Human stubbornness sets requirements that no specification captures in advance, because the people who hold them discover them by using the thing. Adversaries adapt after you ship, which makes security a moving target and not a checklist. Each of these is a source of information the model does not have and cannot be prompted into having.

Two readings of this moment are available, and both are defensible from the same three cost numbers. The first reading is that programming ends as a high-value economic craft, joining weaving, ploughing, and coopering -- skills that were once the centre of an economy and are now hobbies or niches. The second is that new tools open new industries, new research fields, and new careers, as every previous collapse in the cost of a fundamental operation has done.

The first reading is probably directionally right and probably wrong about the clock, and the difference matters more to our staffing plan than the direction does. Consider an expert in analog circuit design who in the late 1960s founded a hardware company with friends, on the bet that serious customers would reject digital logic. The 74 series had just launched, and they found it graceless, unreliable, and full of problems. Measured by the ratio of transistors in digital versus analog circuits in 2026, that bet was wrong by ten or more orders of magnitude. He still made a small fortune, enough to keep his children comfortable, and is now mostly retired. A transition that is total over sixty years pays out slowly enough that a career placed on the losing side of it can still be a good career.

The sticky work and the new work are the same investment, seen from two ends. Integration, translation, simple analysis, and small-scale synthesis are solved, and their market price is heading to the cost of the tokens. Genuinely new ideas are not solved, and they become more valuable as the lever lengthens: an idea that previously took eighteen months of implementation to test now takes a week, which raises the return on having the idea and lowers the return on being able to type it.

So the plan is to stop buying translation capacity and start buying judgment about systems and about problems. Concretely, four changes. Stop sizing teams by the volume of code they are expected to produce, because that quantity no longer measures cost or value. Move headcount toward the end-to-end properties -- latency budgets, failure containment, capacity models, security boundaries -- where the cost curve is still high and still falling slowly. Fund the loop that produces new ideas: time to read, prototypes that are allowed to fail, and contact with the real feedback that only shipping provides. Build the measurement infrastructure that shortens the real-world feedback loop, because that loop is now the long pole and it is the one asset a competitor cannot buy for the price of an API call.

Do not reprice existing skills on a one-year clock. The analog designer's bet was wrong by ten orders of magnitude and it fed his family, because adoption of a better technology is limited by installed base, regulation, training, and habit rather than by the technology's merits. We should move our investment now, and we should expect the people we already have to be productive throughout the move, and we should treat any plan that requires the transition to complete inside three years as a plan that has assumed away the slowest term.
