Concurrency control and consistency defaults

We default to optimistic concurrency control and to strong consistency, because a client that cannot block another client cannot export its own stalls as everybody else's tail latency.

Under pessimistic locking, the lock is a channel between unrelated clients. A process acquires a write lock and then stops for garbage collection, and every transaction queued behind that key stops for the same duration -- not because those transactions are slow, but because one unrelated client's runtime paused. The application sees tail latency with no cause it can find anywhere in its own code. Amazon's operational record names the pattern directly: lock contention, and the retries that follow a failed lock acquisition, turn up repeatedly as causes of outages and of metastable failure. Metastable is the part that matters. A retry after a failed acquisition adds load, added load lengthens the hold times that produced the failure, and the system stays in the bad state after the trigger is gone. Recoveries are long.

Optimistic concurrency control removes that channel instead of shortening it. No transaction waits on another transaction's lock. Conflicts are detected when a transaction tries to commit, and the loser aborts; the winner never waited for anything. A client that pauses for ten seconds holding uncommitted writes delays exactly one transaction, its own. That isolation property, and not throughput alone, is why we chose it. Two design objectives follow from it: keep applications out of metastable failure, and blunt the duller failures that come from badly behaved clients and client code.

The cost of OCC is paid in aborts, by the client that lost, at commit time. Applications must retry aborted transactions, and a workload hammering one row will abort where it would previously have blocked. We take that trade because an abort is bounded work, it surfaces in the application's own error path, and it is uncorrelated with a stranger's GC pause. Aurora DSQL's figure 6 plots SELECT and UPDATE latency under this design, and both stay fast with the database running across regions, which is exactly the case where waiting on a remote lock would cost the most.

Strong consistency belongs as the default now because the premise behind the 2007 answer has expired. The Dynamo paper (2007) and Werner Vogels' Eventually Consistent (2009) held that cloud-scale systems had to give up strong consistency to reach their availability and latency targets, and that was a correct reading of the machinery of 2007. Four things have changed since: how time is distributed, how data-centre networks are built, how power and cooling are provisioned, and what distributed protocols can do.

We therefore ship one mode. There is no eventual-consistency setting to select, because almost no application is better served by one, and an application that reads its own stale write pays a debugging cost that no latency number buys back.
