Twenty years of unwalkable stacks on x86-64 came from copying a register-pressure decision across an ABI boundary where the register pressure no longer existed. The decision itself was reasonable where it was made. In 2004, gcc developer Roger Sayle patched the i386 backend so that 32-bit targets compiled as though `-fomit-frame-pointer -ffixed-ebp` had been passed, which freed %ebp for general use. On i386 that is the difference between four general-purpose registers and five, or six to seven if you count %si and %di, and a 25% increase in the register file shows up on a real benchmark. Sayle gave two other reasons: he wanted to beat Intel's icc, and he judged that the debuggers of the day would be unharmed, since they already supported other ways of walking a stack. Both are defensible readings of 2004 i386.

What did not transfer was the arithmetic. The same default was carried across to x86-64, which already had more than a dozen general-purpose registers, so the change bought one more -- an increase of under 10% against the 25% it bought on i386, and, more to the point, one that arrives after the allocator has stopped spilling. Eric Schrock, then at Sun, made exactly this objection in November 2004: on amd64 a 17th general-purpose register opens up no new class of compiler optimization, all you avoid is a pushl/movl sequence the hardware has long since optimized, and leaf routines never establish a frame at all. Set against the cost in lost debuggability, he wrote, the trade hardly seemed worth making. His closing line named the failure mode precisely -- the practice goes off the rails once people begin compiling /usr/bin without frame pointers.

Linux then did that, and went further, compiling /usr/lib and application code the same way. This is the part of the history that matters for the cost accounting, because the benefit of omitting %rbp is per-function and local, two instructions out of a prologue and epilogue, while the cost is a property of the whole process image. A frame-pointer stack walk is a pointer chase: the walker reads the saved %rbp, follows it to the caller's frame, and repeats. One object in the chain compiled without frame pointers does not degrade the walk, it ends the walk, because %rbp at that point holds whatever the compiler decided to keep there and the next dereference is garbage. So the marginal object file compiled with `-fomit-frame-pointer` does not cost you its own two instructions' worth of visibility -- it costs you every frame below it.

Since libc sits below almost everything, that is where the walk stops in practice. A profiler samples a thread that happens to be inside `read`, `write`, or a mutex acquire, follows %rbp until it reaches libc, and returns a trace with no application frames in it. Those truncated stacks are not discarded, they are aggregated, and because they share a short common prefix they pile up together on the left edge of a flame graph: 15% of the samples in one example, sitting in a stack tower that names no code you wrote. Off-CPU flame graphs come off worse, and the loss there is structural. Off-CPU profiling samples threads that are blocked, blocked threads are overwhelmingly blocked in libc read/write and mutex calls, and that is the same population whose stacks terminate at the first frame.

Recovering visibility took a decade of per-runtime work, which is the clearest evidence that the default was on the wrong side. Java had no frame pointer support at all as of 2014, which broke every application stack on a JVM; the fix was a c2 compiler patch, reworked by Oracle and shipped as `-XX:+PreserveFramePointer` in JDK8u60. libc came next, first as a private build compiled with frame pointers and run in production, then as a prebuilt `libc6-prof` produced with Canonical. Each of those is a separate negotiation with a separate upstream, and neither would have been necessary had the amd64 default been set on amd64's own register arithmetic.

Compile with frame pointers on x86-64. The instruction cost is bounded and known -- one push and one move in the prologue, one pop in the epilogue, absent entirely in leaf functions that never establish a frame, and the published measurements of the aggregate hit on real workloads land around 1%. The benefit is that `perf` and eBPF walk every stack in the process to its application frames without DWARF unwinding, which is the whole basis of a flame graph you can act on. If you are going to argue the other way, argue it with a measurement on your own workload instead of with an inherited default, because the inheritance is the bug: the number that justified omitting %ebp in 2004 was a register count, and that count changed.
