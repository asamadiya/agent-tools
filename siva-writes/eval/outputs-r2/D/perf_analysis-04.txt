Ten percent of a box's CPU spent faulting pages its allocator had just thrown away

A TensorFlow workload was burning 10% of all CPU time on the box handling page faults because jemalloc kept telling the kernel it no longer needed pages the workload was still using, and a rebuild against glibc's allocator brought the first round of testing back at 3%.

Vadim found it by reading a CPU flame graph and picking out an orange kernel tower that did not belong: page-fault work, a tenth of the machine. That number is worth a morning on its own arithmetic. Multiply a tenth of the CPU by a fleet of thousands of instances and you are paying for hundreds of machines that do nothing but rebuild mappings; this team will open a flame graph over a single percentage point, and ten is not a close call.

The faulting stacks led to __memcpy_avx_unaligned(), which means memcpy was touching data segment mappings that were not resident. Early in a process's life that is ordinary and uninteresting, because the segments and the heap have no mappings yet and every first touch is a fault. This process had been running for hours. By that point the growth of RSS from faults should have flattened off, and a process that is still faulting at a steady 10% after hours is either handing memory back to the kernel or being handed it back.

The obvious candidate, a process cycling memory through mmap() and munmap(), did not survive tracing: eBPF with mmapsnoop.py recorded no such calls at all. A flame-graph search on madvise found the real path at 0.8% of CPU, with madvise() descending into zap_page_range(), and that is where the faults were coming from. Reading the kernel's mm/advise.c settled which flag mattered -- MADV_DONTNEED is what sends madvise() down into zap_page_range() -- and tracing sys_madvise() flags with kprobes or tracepoints would have answered the same question without reading any source.

That is an optimization that backfired. The allocator was declaring pages unneeded that the workload still had live, and each dontneed throws away the virtual-to-physical mapping for that range, so the next touch has to trap into the kernel and put a mapping back. The exchange rate is what makes it expensive: the 0.8% of CPU spent issuing the advice bought roughly ten times that much fault handling on the other side, because tearing down a mapping is one cheap call and reconstructing it is a trap, a page walk, and a copy.

The allocator was jemalloc, pulled in by a configure option rather than by anyone deciding this workload wanted jemalloc's purging behavior. Vadim rebuilt against glibc and the problem cleared; testing came back at 3% against the 10% the team had in mind, and the fixed flame graph shows the tower gone.

Check which allocator your build actually links before you trust a page-fault number. A high sustained fault rate on a long-lived process is not a memory-pressure signal by default; it can be your own allocator returning pages it will ask for again, and the flag that does it is visible in one madvise trace.
