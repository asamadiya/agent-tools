BYOIP prefix withdrawal, 20 February 2026

On 20 February 2026 we withdrew roughly 1,100 customer IP prefixes from the Internet, and some of
them stayed withdrawn for five hours. All times are UTC. The outage window runs 17:48 to 23:03,
five hours and fifteen minutes; the withdrawals themselves landed between 17:56 and 18:46. Of the
6,500 prefixes we advertise to a given peer, 4,306 belong to Bring Your Own IP customers, and this
took about 25% of them. The cause was ours, the trigger was a query parameter with no value, and we
are sorry.

What you saw

If your prefix was withdrawn, your traffic did not fail fast. A BGP withdrawal sends routers into
path hunting: each router tries the next-best path it knows, advertises that attempt, and the
attempts propagate before the prefix is finally declared unreachable. Connections hang until
timeout instead of refusing immediately, which is the worst shape a failure can take for anything
with a retry loop behind it.

Visitors to the one.one.one.one website received HTTP 403 with an Edge IP Restricted message. DNS
resolution over 1.1.1.1, including DNS over HTTPS, kept working throughout the incident and was not
part of this failure.

What happened

We had added a sub-task to automate removal of BYOIP prefixes already queued for deletion. It
called the Addressing API with GET /v1/prefixes?pending_delete -- key present, value absent. In Go,
Query().Get("pending_delete") returns an empty string for that, and the API read an empty filter as
no filter and returned every BYOIP prefix in the system. The sub-task took that response as its
work queue and began deleting, prefix by prefix, along with the service bindings that depend on
them. The Addressing API is the authoritative source for customer IP addresses and pushes changes
to the edge immediately, so each deletion reached the routers within seconds. Nothing in that path
asked whether removing a quarter of our BYOIP prefixes in fifty minutes was plausible.

Timeline

The change merged on 5 February at 21:53 and sat unreleased for fifteen days. Deployment began at
17:46 on 20 February and the first withdrawals followed at 17:56. We engaged on the wrong signal
first: at 18:13 engineers picked up the one.one.one.one failures, which are a downstream symptom of
the deletions. We declared an internal incident at 18:18 and paged the Addressing API team at
18:21. An engineer terminated the sub-process at 18:46, fifty minutes after the first withdrawal.
Mitigation was in place at 19:11 and we published self-remediation guidance at 19:19. About 800
prefixes were re-advertised by 20:20. The remaining ~300 had lost service bindings that our
automated restore could not reconstruct, and we restored those by hand, finishing at 23:03.

Why our tests did not catch it

Our tests covered the customer self-service journey: a person clicks delete, one prefix goes away,
the right bindings are torn down. They did not cover a task runner mutating customer data with no
explicit input from a customer. Staging made the gap invisible from the other side -- with a
handful of fixture prefixes and mocked dependencies, an unfiltered list and a correctly filtered
list look close enough that both pass. The number of rows returned is the entire signal here, and
we had arranged for that number to be small in every environment where we looked at it.

What we are changing

Reject empty filter values. A request to /v1/prefixes with a filter key and no value returns 400
instead of the full table.

Bound destructive automation. An automated task that would delete more than a small fraction of a
customer-visible resource class halts and requires human confirmation before it proceeds.

Stage prefix-affecting deletions. Deletions originating from automated tasks propagate to the edge
on a delay with a window to detect and abort, instead of landing within seconds.

Test task runners as first-class callers, against production-shaped prefix data in staging instead
of mocks. A test that cannot tell 3 rows from 4,306 is not testing the filter.

Alert on withdrawal rate directly. We detected this through a 403 on a website, 17 minutes after
the first prefix left the table. Withdrawing ~1,100 prefixes in fifty minutes should page us on its
own evidence.

The expensive assumption here was not the missing filter value. It was that a batch job deleting
customer resources needs no more supervision than a customer deleting one of their own.
