A tenth of the CPU on the box was going into page faults, and nothing about the workload explained it. Vadim was reading a CPU flame graph from a TensorFlow job and picked out an orange tower of kernel fault-handling work standing at 10% of all cycles on the instance. Ten percent would be worth an afternoon on one machine, but the arithmetic that makes it urgent is the fleet: the same job runs on thousands of instances, so a tenth of each one is hundreds of instances' worth of compute bought and then spent on bookkeeping. We will open a flame graph over a single percentage point, and this was ten.

The faulting stacks led to __memcpy_avx_unaligned(), which puts the fault at memcpy touching data-segment mappings that were not resident. That is completely ordinary in the first seconds of a process, when the segments and the heap have no mappings behind them yet and every first touch costs a fault: RSS climbs, the fault rate tapers, and the tower falls out of the profile. This process had been up for hours, by which point the growth of RSS from faults should have flattened off. Something inside it was still handing mappings back at steady state.

The usual candidate for steady-state faulting is a process cycling memory through mmap() and munmap(), and eBPF tracing with mmapsnoop.py recorded no such calls. A flame-graph search on madvise found it instead, at 0.8% of CPU, with the madvise() path descending into zap_page_range(), which is the routine that tears down the page table entries for an address range. Reading the kernel's madvise implementation in mm/madvise.c settled which flag mattered: MADV_DONTNEED is what sends madvise() into zap_page_range(). Trace the flags to sys_madvise() with a kprobe or a tracepoint and you get the same answer with more setup and no kernel source in front of you.

That makes this an optimization that backfired. The allocator was declaring pages unneeded that the workload still had live data in; each MADV_DONTNEED threw away the virtual-to-physical mapping, and the next touch of that address took a fault so the kernel could put the mapping back. The allocator turned out to be jemalloc, pulled in by a configure option. Vadim rebuilt against glibc and the tower came down: the first round of testing put page-fault work at 3% of CPU against the 10% the team had in mind, and the fixed flame graph shows the difference directly.

I would hold 3% loosely. It is one round of testing on one workload, and some part of what remains is the ordinary faulting any process does, so the honest claim is that the regression is gone rather than that 3% is the floor.

The reusable part is the accounting error. The caller that created this cost appeared in the profile at 0.8%, an eighth of what it was costing, because the expensive half of the transaction was charged to the fault that happened later, under memcpy, in a stack that named nothing about allocation. A cheap-looking frame next to an unexplained kernel tower is worth reading as one thing.
