Impact analysis: downstream services
(All times UTC.)

Workers KV was the shared dependency behind most of what enterprise customers saw. The KV front-end gateway issues its requests through the core proxy, and the core proxy was failing, which pushed KV itself into heavily elevated 5xx for the length of the incident. KV was not broken internally; it was returning the failure of the thing it calls. At 13:04 we shipped a patch that let the KV gateway bypass the core proxy entirely, and error rates fell across everything sitting downstream of KV, Access included.

Cloudflare Access failed authentication widely from the start of the incident until the rollback at 13:05. Existing Access sessions were unaffected, so the population that felt this was users authenticating for the first time or re-authenticating during the window. The failure mode matters for your security review: every failed authentication terminated at an error page, which means no user reached a target application while authentication was broken, and every login that did succeed during the window was logged correctly. We have found no case of a user being admitted past a failed check. Access configuration updates attempted during the window either failed outright or propagated very slowly; all of them have since been recovered, and no configuration change made during the window has been silently dropped.

The Dashboard was unavailable across two separate windows, for two different reasons. The Dashboard depends on Workers KV internally and on Turnstile in its login flow. Turnstile failed to load, which left anyone without an active session unable to log in at all. The first window ran 11:30 to 13:10 and ended when the KV bypass took effect. The second window ran 14:40 to 15:30 and was our own doing: once the feature file was restored, a backlog of queued login attempts arrived together with client retries, and the control plane could not absorb the combined rate. Scaling control plane concurrency around 15:30 ended it. The second window is the more interesting one for us, because the first was caused by the incident and the second was caused by recovering from it.

Email Security continued to process and deliver mail throughout. Detection quality degraded instead of delivery: the service lost access to one IP reputation source, which reduced spam-detection accuracy and suppressed some new-domain-age detections. Some Auto Move actions failed during the window. Those have been remediated. Customers evaluating exposure should treat mail that arrived during the incident window as having been scanned by a detection pipeline missing one signal, not as unscanned.

CDN response latency rose for reasons unrelated to any of the above. Our debugging and observability systems enrich uncaught errors before emitting them, and the volume of uncaught errors during the incident drove that enrichment work to consume large amounts of CPU on the serving path. The observability tooling competed with the traffic it was observing. We are treating that as a first-class finding: a debugging path that costs more the worse things get is a latency amplifier precisely when latency matters most.

Two boundaries are worth stating plainly, because customers have asked. Existing Access sessions held throughout, and authentication failed closed rather than open. Mail was delivered, not queued or lost. The recoverable damage was concentrated in Access configuration propagation and in failed Auto Move actions, and both sets have been recovered and remediated respectively.
