Uploads that take hours at 100 Gbytes and minutes at 40 Gbytes

The shape of the report is what made this worth chasing. A service that encrypts large files and ships them to S3 finished 40-Gbyte uploads in minutes and ran for hours on files of roughly 100 Gbytes. That is 2.5x the input for something north of 20x the runtime, depending on how generously you read "minutes" and "hours," and a curve that bends that hard usually means the workload crosses a threshold somewhere between the two sizes instead of degrading smoothly with size. The other thing that moved was pageins in Atlas, our cloud-wide monitoring, which count pages faulted in off disk. Plenty of healthy workloads page in, so that signal narrows the search without deciding anything.

Running the 60-second checklist during a large upload, iostat -xz 1 shows device xvdb saturated and the latency sitting in the queue rather than in the device. Over the sampled seconds: 734 to 750 r/s, 91,656 to 92,152 rkB/s, avgrq-sz 246 to 250 sectors, avgqu-sz about 25, r_await 33 to 34 ms, %util pegged at 100. Divide the queue depth by the completion rate and you get 25 / 742 = 33.7 ms, which is r_await to within the noise in the samples -- Little's law closing on itself, which is what it looks like when essentially all of the wait is time spent behind other requests. Back out the per-I/O service time and it is 33.7 / 25 = 1.35 ms, and 742 I/O per second at 1.35 ms each is 100% busy. The device is doing about 92 Mbytes/s and it is doing it flat out.

The I/O are large, and two independent fields in the same output say so. rkB/s divided by r/s gives 92,152 / 750 = 123 kbytes, and avgrq-sz of 246 to 250 sectors at 512 bytes gives 126 to 128 kbytes. Call it 128 kbytes, which is the largest size the block layer will hand down here without extra configuration, meaning this is sequential-looking bulk reading and not a scattered random pattern that happens to be slow.

Reads matter here and writes mostly do not. Applications block on reads; writes usually land in the page cache and are flushed later, so a write-heavy profile with the same numbers would be much less alarming. A 33 ms read wait against a 1.35 ms service time means the application is waiting on a 25-deep queue of its own making.

CPU rules out the encryption. Across the same seconds the box runs about 14.8% user and 24 to 30% iowait on 16 CPUs, so roughly 2.4 CPUs' worth of user work. If AES over a 100-Gbyte file were the constraint we would expect user time several times that, and iowait -- idle time with I/O outstanding -- is the complement of the queueing already visible in iostat, not new information.

biolatency -m from bcc was the next step, in case per-second averages were hiding device-level outliers, and it found none. Of 10,267 I/O, 8,139 landed at 32-63 ms and another 1,620 at 16-31 ms, so 95% of the distribution sits in two adjacent buckets straddling the 33 ms average, with 176 at 64-127 ms and a thin tail of 95, 61, and 93 I/O reaching 0.5-1.0 s. Eighty-three I/O completed in under 1 ms. A device with a real problem gives a bimodal histogram with a fat slow mode; this one gives a single mode where queueing theory says it should be.

bitesize agreed and named the process: java, 1,682 I/O in the 128-255 kbyte bucket, tens in every other bucket.

Nothing here indicts the disk. The reading is workload, not device -- and the workload is the odd part, because 100 Gbytes at a sustained 92 Mbytes/s is 1,090 seconds, about 18 minutes, which is not hours. Either the reads are not sustained, or the process is reading the file more than once. The next section measures which.
