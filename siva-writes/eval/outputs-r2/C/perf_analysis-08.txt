Frame pointer overhead

With frame pointers compiled into everything -- libc and the JVM both -- the cost we measured across the Netflix fleet normally came in under 1%, and on most services it was small enough that we could not separate it from run-to-run variance at all. That is the headline, and it is the number I would use for planning if you are deciding whether to rebuild your distro with -fno-omit-frame-pointer. One workload broke the pattern at 10%, which is worth going through in detail, both because it is the outlier that people cite at us and because the way it resists analysis is more interesting than the number.

The 10% service generated stack traces more than 1000 frames deep through Groovy, deep enough that Linux's perf profiler could not walk them: perf had a hard cap on stack depth, and Arnaldo Carvalho de Melo at Red Hat added a new sysctl, kernel.perf_event_max_stack, specifically so that this one Netflix workload could be profiled at all. A stack that deep means every function entry and exit is paying the prologue and epilogue on a call chain a thousand deep, so an overhead that is invisible on a normal service has a thousand opportunities per request to show up, and a 10% result stops being surprising. What I cannot tell you is that all 10% came from frame pointers. The instance was a virtual machine with no low-level hardware profiling available, so there was no way to run cycle analysis, attribute cycles to the prologue instructions, and close the loop. We have a plausible mechanism and a measured delta and no proof that they are the same thing.

How much it costs depends on the workload, and that is not a hedge, it is the actual finding. Independent reports from outside Netflix have come in at roughly 1% and roughly 2%, which brackets our own fleet result, and the reports that come in high are almost always microbenchmarks. Microbenchmarks sit at the bad end structurally: a microbenchmark reduces to one small function executed in a loop, the whole working set is L1-resident by construction, and any instructions you add to that function risk pushing it past L1 cache warmth or over a cache line boundary, at which point throughput falls off a step rather than degrading smoothly. A real service has a working set that is already missing cache for reasons that have nothing to do with %rbp, so the marginal cost of a few more bytes of instruction encoding is buried. If you get a 10% microbenchmark result and want to know whether it generalizes, the way to find out is cycles, instructions, PMU counters, PMCs and PEBS -- not the headline ratio, which tells you a number changed and nothing about why.

Python produced the other 10% outlier, and that one was run all the way down. scimark_sparse_mat_mult regressed about 10%, and Andrii Nakryiko at Meta traced it to a single large function where gcc, having lost %rbp as a general register, switched from %rsp-relative addressing to offsets computed off %rbp. The %rbp form needs more bytes to encode, so the function grew, and the cost was instruction bytes in a hot loop rather than the prologue and epilogue everyone assumes they are paying for. That is a codegen bug rather than an inherent property of frame pointers, it has since been fixed, and the fix is what lets Python turn frame pointers back on by default. The two 10% results have nothing in common except the number: one is a thousand-deep stack paying real prologue cost, the other was a compiler making a bad addressing choice under register pressure.

Set the cost against what the profiles find. Working continuous profiling across a fleet turns up performance wins in the 5% to 500% range, and the low end of that range already pays for a sub-1% tax several times over. Even taking the pessimistic reading -- assume every service costs 1% to 2%, assume the 10% cases are not as rare as we think -- you are trading a fixed, bounded, measurable overhead for the ability to see where cycles actually go, and you only need to find one 5% win per service to break even. Zero overhead would be better, and SFrame and similar unwinding technologies may eventually get there. Today nothing that is actually available finds those wins as reliably as a frame pointer walk does.
