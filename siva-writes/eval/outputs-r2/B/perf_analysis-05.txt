## A 100-Gbyte upload that took hours

A service team came to us in December 2018 with a complaint that had a suspicious shape: uploads of files around 100 Gbytes ran for hours, while uploads of files up to 40 Gbytes finished in minutes. The service encrypts large files and ships them to S3, and it does that on a 16-CPU AWS instance running kernel 4.4.0-1072-aws. A 2.5x increase in file size was buying far more than a 2.5x increase in runtime, which rules out a plain throughput ceiling and points at something that changes state between those two sizes.

Atlas, our cloud-wide monitoring tool, showed a pageins spike lining up with the slow uploads. Pageins count pages faulted in from disk. Plenty of healthy workloads generate them -- any process that mmaps a file and touches it generates them -- so this is a place to start looking, not a verdict.

We started from the 60-second performance checklist. `iostat -xz 1` during a large upload, device xvdb, held steady across the sampled seconds: r/s 734 to 750, rkB/s 91,656 to 92,152, avgrq-sz 246 to 250, avgqu-sz about 25, r_await 33 to 34 ms, %util pegged at 100. CPU over the same seconds ran about 14.8% user with 24 to 30% iowait.

Read the wait apart from the service time before calling 33 ms slow. Reads are the operations an application blocks on; writes frequently are not, because write-back caching returns before the bytes land. But 33 ms of read wait against an average queue depth of 25 is arithmetic, not pathology: 25 requests in flight divided by 740 completions per second gives 33.8 ms of residency per request, which is r_await to within the rounding of the sample. Invert the completion rate and the device is spending about 1.35 ms of its own time per I/O. Thirty-two of the thirty-four milliseconds are the request sitting behind twenty-four others. Divide rkB/s by r/s and each of those I/O carries about 124 Kbytes, so the device is returning roughly 92 Mbytes/s while saturated -- a disk doing its job under a deep queue.

Per-second averages can hide device-level outliers, so the next tool was `biolatency -m` from bcc, an eBPF histogram of block I/O latency. The buckets: 83 I/O at 0-1 ms, 1,620 at 16-31 ms, 8,139 at 32-63 ms, 176 at 64-127 ms, 95 at 128-255 ms, 61 at 256-511 ms, 93 at 512-1023 ms. That is 10,267 I/O total, with 79.3% of them landing in the single 32-63 ms bucket that iostat's average already described, and 0.9% out in the half-second-to-second tail. A device with a firmware stall or a failing sector produces a bimodal histogram with a fat slow mode. This one produces a mode sitting exactly where a 25-deep queue predicts, plus a tail that is what a 25-deep queue does to an unlucky request.

`bitesize` from bcc checked the size distribution for anything anomalous. Process name java, 1,682 I/O in the 128-255 Kbyte bucket, and only tens in every other bucket. The requests are large, uniform, and consistent with the 124 Kbyte average computed from iostat. No small-random-read pathology, no fragmentation signature.

Three tools agree, and none of them accuses the storage device. xvdb is being asked for about 740 large reads per second by a Java process, it is answering them at 92 Mbytes/s, and it is 100% busy because the queue never drains. The question worth asking next is why the upload is reading from disk at this rate at all. Streaming a 100-Gbyte file once at 92 Mbytes/s takes about 18 minutes of read time. The job takes hours, which means the bytes are crossing the disk boundary several times over, and the 40-Gbyte case does not do that.
