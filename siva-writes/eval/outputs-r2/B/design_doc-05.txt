Transaction layer defaults: optimistic concurrency, strong consistency

We propose two defaults for the new transactional datastore: optimistic concurrency control for all transactions, and strong consistency for all reads. Both follow the choices Aurora DSQL made for its multi-region relational engine, and both are defaults an operator can reason about before the first incident.

Optimistic concurrency control buys throughput and one isolation property we care about more: a client can never put itself in a position where it blocks another client. A transaction reads, computes, and submits; the commit either succeeds or is rejected at validation. No transaction holds a resource that another transaction must wait on, which removes the mechanism behind client-to-client performance cross talk. That cross talk drives tail latency in every application sitting on a pessimistic relational engine, because one client's behaviour becomes every other client's latency.

The failure is easiest to see in the stock illustration. A process pauses for garbage collection while holding a write lock. Everything queued behind that lock stops for the duration of the pause, and the queue keeps growing while the holder is descheduled. Under optimistic control the paused process delays only itself, and its transaction is rejected at validation if the world moved underneath it.

Amazon's operational record is the stronger argument. Lock contention, together with the retry storms that follow a failed lock acquisition, appears repeatedly as a cause of outages and of metastable failure, and those incidents recover slowly because the retries sustain the overload that produced them. Two design objectives fall out of that record: keep applications out of metastable failure at all, and blunt the duller failures that badly behaved clients and client code produce on a shared engine.

The cost of optimistic control is a rejected commit under write-write conflict, and a workload with a single hot row will pay it. We accept that. Figure 6 of the DSQL paper plots SELECT and UPDATE latency and both stay fast with the database running across regions, which is the case where a pessimistic engine would hold locks across a wide-area round trip.

Strong consistency is the second default, and it reverses a position the field inherited. The Dynamo paper (2007) and Werner Vogels' Eventually Consistent (2009) argued that cloud-scale systems had to surrender strong consistency to reach their availability and latency targets. Four things have changed since: how time is distributed, how data-centre networks are built, how power and cooling are provisioned, and what distributed protocols can do. Strong consistency now belongs in the default path, and we have found almost no application that is better served by eventual consistency once the reconciliation logic it forces on the application is counted.
