## What frame pointers actually cost

Across the Netflix rollout, with frame pointers compiled into everything we ship -- libc and Java both, not just the application layer -- the cost normally landed under 1%. On many services it was too small to measure at all: the difference between the frame-pointer build and the omit-frame-pointer build sat inside the run-to-run noise, and no amount of repetition separated them. That is the number to carry around as the default expectation. It is not the only number, and the exceptions are worth more attention than the average, because they tell you which of your own workloads will disagree.

One service broke the pattern at 10%. It was an unusual one: stack traces more than 1000 frames deep, generated through Groovy, deep enough that Linux's perf profiler could not walk them. Arnaldo Carvalho de Melo at Red Hat added a new sysctl, `kernel.perf_event_max_stack`, specifically so that this one Netflix workload could be profiled at all. The same instance was a virtual machine with no low-level hardware profiling available, which is the part that keeps the 10% from being a clean result. Without cycle analysis we could not establish that all 10% came from frame pointers, or how much came from everything else that changed in that build. Treat it as an upper bound on one workload, measured with the coarsest instrument available.

Independent reports from outside Netflix have come in at roughly 1% and roughly 2%. Those bracket the honest answer for a real service under real traffic. How much it costs depends on the workload, and the shape of the dependence is not mysterious: the cost is one extra push, one extra pop, and one register removed from the allocator's pool, paid per function call. A service that spends its time in system calls, in memory stalls, or in a few hot loops that the compiler has already inlined will not notice. A service that spends its time in a deep tree of small, frequently called functions pays the most.

Microbenchmarks sit at the bad end of that range and can show 10%. This is unsurprising once you look at what a microbenchmark is. It reduces to one small function executed in a loop. Any instructions added to that function risk pushing it past L1 cache warmth or over a cache line boundary, at which point throughput drops -- and the drop is attributed to the frame pointer, when the frame pointer only supplied the last few bytes that crossed the line. The benchmark is measuring an alignment cliff in a single function, not the cost of a calling convention across a program.

So do not judge a microbenchmark by its headline number. Open it with observability analysis: cycles, instructions, PMU counters, PMCs, PEBS. Count the instructions retired and the cycles spent, compute IPC for both builds, and check whether the front end started missing. If the instruction count moved by a fraction of a percent and the cycle count moved by 10%, the extra instructions are not the mechanism and you should go looking for the cache line.

Python produced the other outlier worth reading. The `scimark_sparse_mat_mult` benchmark also touched 10%, and Andrii Nakryiko at Meta ran it down to a specific compiler behaviour rather than to the calling convention. In one large function, gcc had switched from `%rsp`-relative addressing to offsets computed off `%rbp`, and the `%rbp` form needs more bytes to encode. A bigger function body, more instruction bytes fetched, and a 10% regression that has nothing to do with the push and pop everyone assumes they are paying for. That has since been fixed, which is what let Python turn frame pointers back on by default.

Now set these costs against what frame pointers find. Working profiles on production services surface wins in the range of 5% to 500% -- a badly sized thread pool, a hot path through a serializer nobody suspected, a lock held across an I/O call. A standing cost under 1%, or even 1 to 2% on the services that sit at the unfavourable end, is easily paid for by finding one of those per quarter. Zero overhead would be better, and later unwinding technologies may get there. For now, nothing else available finds those wins as reliably on a fleet you did not build yesterday.
