Twenty years of missing stacks: how the frame pointer left x86-64

The x86-64 ABI leaves the frame pointer optional, and a default chosen in 2004 for a different
architecture is why a flame graph collected on a modern Linux box comes back without its
application frames. %rbp holds the base of the current stack frame. A profiler that walks stacks
from outside the process -- Linux perf, eBPF -- reads the saved %rbp at a known offset, jumps to
the caller's frame, and repeats until the chain ends; that walk is what produces the stacks a
flame graph aggregates. Code compiled to index into its frame off %rsp instead keeps no such
chain. It saves two instructions per function, one in the prologue and one in the epilogue, and
hands one general-purpose register back to the allocator.

Roger Sayle patched gcc's i386 backend in 2004 so that 32-bit targets compiled as though
-fomit-frame-pointer -ffixed-ebp had been passed on the command line. On i386 the register was
worth taking. Four allocatable general-purpose registers became five, or six to seven counting
%si and %di, on a machine where register pressure decided how often a hot loop spilled to memory.
Sayle gave two further reasons. He wanted gcc to beat Intel's icc on benchmarks, and he judged
that the debuggers of the day would be unharmed, since they already supported other ways of
walking a stack.

The same default then carried across to x86-64, a target that already had more than a dozen
general-purpose registers. Run the arithmetic on both machines and the trade stops matching. One
more register on top of four widens the allocator's working set by a quarter; one more on top of
sixteen changes very little about which values live in registers across a call. The two
instructions saved in the prologue and epilogue are the same two instructions on either target,
and on the wider one they are the whole of the benefit.

Eric Schrock, then at Sun, objected in November 2004, and his argument was specific to amd64. A
17th general-purpose register, he wrote, opens up no new class of compiler optimization. The
pushl/movl sequence it avoids is one the hardware has long since optimized. Leaf routines never
establish a frame at all, which means the most common functions in a hot profile pay nothing
either way. Set against the cost in lost debuggability, he concluded, the trade hardly seemed
worth making. His closing line named the failure mode precisely: the practice goes off the rails
once people begin compiling /usr/bin without frame pointers.

Linux then compiled /usr/bin without frame pointers, and not only /usr/bin. /usr/lib went the
same way, and so did application code. The decision was never made once per binary by someone
weighing a profile against a benchmark; it arrived as a distribution-wide compiler default and
propagated to everything built under it.

The cost shows up the moment a stack walk crosses into libc. The walk reaches a frame with no
saved %rbp to follow, the chain ends there, and the trace returns holding the libc frames and
nothing above them. Those truncated stacks are not spread across the profile. They share a common
prefix, they merge into one tower, and they pile up together on the left edge of a flame graph --
15% of the samples in one example, a sixth of the profile reporting only that the process was
somewhere inside the C library. Off-CPU flame graphs come off worse. libc read and write calls
and mutex operations are most of what an off-CPU profile contains, which puts the truncation
exactly where the interesting blocking lives.

Java broke a second way, and that became the first repair. On joining Netflix in 2014, the author
found that the JVM had no frame pointer support of its own, so every application stack was
unwalkable regardless of how libc had been built. He wrote a fix in the c2 compiler. Oracle
reworked it and shipped it as -XX:+PreserveFramePointer in JDK8u60, which is the flag a Java
profile on Linux still depends on today.

libc came next. He built his own with frame pointers for production use, which works and does not
travel: a locally compiled libc solves the problem for one fleet and leaves every other
installation walking off the same cliff. The durable version of that fix was a package other
people could install, and he worked with Canonical to produce a prebuilt one, libc6-prof.

Trace the line from 2004 to 2014 and the shape of the mistake is visible. A measured win on a
register-starved 32-bit target became a default on a target that was not register-starved, the
default became distribution policy, and the policy quietly removed the mechanism that external
profilers use to attribute time to application code. Schrock wrote down the consequence before
it happened, in the month the change spread, and it took a decade of per-component repairs to
begin undoing it.
