Ten percent of a box, spent on page faults

Vadim was reading a CPU flame graph from a TensorFlow workload when an orange tower caught his eye: page-fault handling, 10% of all CPU time on the instance. Page faults are supposed to be a startup cost, not a standing tax. A tenth of the CPU on one machine is a rounding error you could live with; a tenth of the CPU across a fleet of thousands of instances is a line item. We will open a flame graph over one percentage point, and this was ten.

The faulting stacks ran down into __memcpy_avx_unaligned(). That is memcpy touching data segment mappings that were not resident. Early in a process's life this is completely ordinary, because the segments and the heap have no mappings yet and every first touch costs a fault. This process had been up for hours. By that point RSS growth from faulting should have flattened, and the fault rate with it.

One standard explanation for sustained faulting is a process cycling memory through mmap() and munmap(), returning address space to the kernel and asking for it back. eBPF tracing with mmapsnoop.py recorded no such calls, which killed that theory outright. Search the same flame graph for madvise instead. It came back at 0.8% of CPU, with the madvise() path descending into zap_page_range() -- and zap_page_range() is where the faults were being manufactured. Reading mm/advise.c in the kernel source settled which flag was responsible: MADV_DONTNEED is the one that routes madvise() into zap_page_range(). Tracing the sys_madvise() flags directly with kprobes or a tracepoint would have answered the same question from the other direction, and either route lands on the same flag.

That gives the diagnosis: an optimization eating itself. The allocator was declaring pages unneeded while the workload was still using them. Each MADV_DONTNEED threw away the virtual-to-physical mapping for those pages, and the next access took a page fault to rebuild exactly the mapping that had just been discarded. The allocator was paying the kernel to undo its own work, and the memcpy stacks were the bill arriving.

The allocator was jemalloc, pulled into the build by a configure option that nobody in the loop had set deliberately. jemalloc's decay-based purging is a good default for a lot of workloads; it is a bad default for one that keeps touching the pages it just freed. Vadim rebuilt against glibc's allocator and the pattern cleared. The first round of testing put page-fault work at 3% of CPU, against the 10% we started with, and the corrected flame graph shows the tower shrunk to a stub.

Three things generalize. A fault rate that does not decay with process age is a bug, not warm-up. A 0.8% tower is worth clicking on when a 10% tower has no other explanation, because the cheap caller can be driving the expensive callee. And an allocator swap is a one-line configure change with a seven-point CPU consequence, which makes it worth knowing which allocator your binary actually linked.
