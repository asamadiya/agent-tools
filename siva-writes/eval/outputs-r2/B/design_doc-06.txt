Where the Value Moves: Engineering Investment, 2026-2036

Three costs have moved, and they have not moved together. Turning written business logic into code now costs approximately zero: a competent engineer with a competent model converts a specification paragraph into a tested function in the time it takes to read the paragraph. Integrating services and libraries also costs approximately zero. The client, the retry wrapper, the schema adapter, the glue that consumed a large share of every sprint for two decades, is now generated on demand. Building efficient, reliable, secure, end-to-end systems is getting cheaper too, but slowly, and the gap between "slowly" and "approximately zero" is the whole subject of this document.

Two readings of that moment circulate in this organisation, and both are defensible. The first says programming is ending as a high-value economic craft, joining weaving, ploughing, and coopering. The second says new tools open new industries, new research fields, and new careers. We plan against the second reading while respecting the first, and the reason is arithmetic rather than optimism.

Amdahl's Law names the mechanism. Speed up one portion of a problem and that portion becomes a shrinking share of total time; the untouched portion grows as a fraction of what remains. Apply it to a project rather than a processor. If translation and plumbing were 60% of delivery and now cost near zero, the remaining 40% is 100% of the schedule, and every further improvement to code generation buys a diminishing slice of nothing. Measure your own projects before accepting those fractions. Take three shipped efforts, tag each engineer-week as specification, plumbing, or system-building, and compute the ratio. We expect the system-building share to exceed 70% on any project that reached production this year.

The residual is not a rounding error, and it is worth naming what sits in it. Real-world feedback loops, where a system changes the behaviour it measures. Human stubbornness, which no interface removes. Occasional adversaries, who are optimising against you in real time and are not sampling from your training distribution. Integration, translation, simple analysis, and small-scale synthesis are solved. Genuinely new ideas are not, and they grow more valuable as the lever lengthens: an idea that would have taken nine months to build and test now takes three weeks, which raises the return on having the idea and lowers the return on the hands that would have built it.

A caution about timing, drawn from a real bet. In the late 1960s an analog circuit designer founded a hardware company with friends on the wager that serious customers would reject digital logic. The 74 series had just launched, and to them it looked graceless and unreliable. Measured by the ratio of transistors in digital versus analog circuits in 2026, that bet was wrong by ten or more orders of magnitude. He still made a small fortune, enough to keep his children comfortable, because the world changes slower than anyone would like. Read that twice, in both directions. Being directionally wrong by ten orders of magnitude cost him one career and no more; being directionally right ten years early would have cost him the company. Our planning horizon is a decade because that is the interval over which the direction is legible and the timing is not.

Four investments follow.

Invest in systems competence, not code volume. Fund the skills that price the residual: distributed correctness, failure-mode analysis, capacity modelling, security review, and the operational judgement that decides what to page a human about at 3am. These transfer across every stack we run and they are the scarce input now.

Invest in evaluation infrastructure ahead of generation infrastructure. Cheap code raises the number of candidate changes per engineer by an order of magnitude, and the constraint moves to deciding which candidates are correct. Budget for test environments, replay corpora, and measurement harnesses at a ratio we should set explicitly: we propose two engineer-weeks of evaluation capacity per ten engineer-weeks of feature work, revisited at each half.

Invest in problem selection. Fewer, harder problems beat more, easier ones when the build cost collapses. Kill work that a competitor can now reproduce in a fortnight, and route the freed headcount toward problems that require our data, our operating history, or our customer access.

Hold integration and translation flat. Do not staff a team to build what a model now writes in an afternoon. Existing owners absorb it.

The organisation that keeps its judgement about which systems are worth building will be busy in 2036. The measurable commitment for the next two quarters is the tagging exercise above: three projects, engineer-weeks classified into three buckets, results reviewed against the 70% expectation before we set the next headcount plan.
